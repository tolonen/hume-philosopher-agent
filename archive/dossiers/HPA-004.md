# HPA-004: Garrett, Millican and the scope of reason in induction

Date: 12 October 2026. **Verdict: qualified positive support for Garrett; categorical bad-argument refutation withdrawn.** This is a completed assessment of an established disagreement, not an original intervention.

## Target, context and access

The [Garrett–Millican 2011 dialogue](https://era.ed.ac.uk/bitstreams/ce5776f7-d5c1-4972-bc50-4952a296ac36/download), pp. 17,20–24, tests whether unsuccessful argument can explain inductive belief. Garrett's p. 24 distinction is exact: “The bad part is not some demonstration.” He permits fallible probable reasoning and complex reflective inference. The target is therefore the classification of an indispensable transition and of a failed demonstrative step, not a denial that humans reason badly.

The complete dialogue and [Garrett's reply to Millican](https://humesociety.org/?hume_article_pdf=16383), *Hume Studies*40(2),227–242 (issue2014, appeared2016), were read by the separate investigator. The later reply's pp. 234–235 and notes6–7 matter decisively. Selective original inspection of Millican 2012/2014, Loeb 2006, Qu and Cottrell 2024 provides objections and novelty coverage; it does not amount to complete evaluation of those works. Exact reading boundaries, quotes and URLs are in the [full assessment](../notes/garrett-induction-assessment-v2.md) and [ledger](../notes/garrett-induction-assessment-v2-sources.json).

## Argument tested

Distinguish three hypotheses:

1. Every ordinary prediction needs a prior argument establishing uniformity.
2. Every inductive episode depends on custom for its indispensable transition or conviction.
3. No explicit argument ever contributes proximately to a prediction.

Hume's infants and animals oppose1. They do not establish3. The serious question is whether2 explains both ordinary and reflective cases, and how this bears on the boundary of reason.

**Primary evidence:** [T1.3.6.4–16](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/6), SBN88–94, moves from understanding/imagination to the failure of rational production and then to association. [T1.3.8.13–14](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/8), SBN103–105, expressly handles reflection and the single experiment by reinstating custom indirectly. [T1.3.9.19n22](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/9), SBN117–118, gives an inferential boundary of narrow imagination. [T1.4.4.1](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/4), SBN225, evaluates permanent and irregular imaginative principles. [T1.4.7.7](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/7), SBN267–268, is the difficult apparent identification of understanding with established imaginative properties. The complete relevant sections were read, not isolated search hits.

## Explicit inference and positive result

**P1 — textual:** the habitual transition belongs to imagination rather than reason in T1.3.6.

**P2 — textual:** such transitions belong to the stable, indispensable class in T1.4.4.

**P3 — rival interpretive premise:** narrow imagination is simply the irregular, epistemically suspect class.

**Inference:** P1 and P2 put pressure on P3. A process can be non-argumentative yet stable and indispensable. The inferential boundary and the evaluative boundary therefore need not coincide.

**Positive alternative:** Garrett's separation of these boundaries explains both assignments. It also fits the one-experiment case: reflection can direct a prediction while a habitually grounded general principle contributes the conviction. Calling this a wholly reason-free episode would be stronger than the evidence; locating an indispensable customary basis is well supported.

The result matters: excluding a mechanism from reason does not automatically condemn its epistemic contribution. Conversely, a good epistemic contribution does not automatically make its mechanism demonstrative or probable reasoning. This removes a false forced choice between psychological explanation and epistemic evaluation while giving qualified support to Garrett within the actual dispute.

## Strongest objection, defence and residual weakness

Millican's serious challenge remains: an apparently valid but actually invalid demonstration can persuade. An explanation of belief must explain that causal route. [T1.4.1.1](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/1), SBN180, distinguishes infallible rules from fallible performance; [E5.22](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/5), SBN55, explicitly speaks of fallacious deductions of reason. Neither licenses classifying every invalid performance as non-reason by terminology alone.

Garrett's defence is stronger than the initial objection allowed. He permits bad probable reasoning and distinguishes operations within an attempted demonstration. E5.22 therefore does not by itself contradict him; its deductions need not be exclusively would-be demonstrations. His later reply directly addresses stable and irregular imagination. An objection alleging that he simply ignores these passages is withdrawn.

The residual cost is real. Treating T1.4.7.7's apparent identification as a claim about *dependence* requires interpretive explanation; the footnote's taxonomy also has an acknowledged intuition complication. We have not found a passage expressly assigning the faulty step of a persuasive apparent demonstration to narrow imagination. Thus qualified support for the two-boundary reading does not establish Garrett's strongest possible exclusion of invalid argumentative performances.

## Chronology, novelty and verdict changes

Treatise arguments are assessed first. E4–5 are later corroboration and pressure tests, not automatic evidence of unchanged intention. The later scholar reply supersedes criticisms answered there; its issue and actual publication dates are recorded separately. Garrett 1998 and his2015 book were not fully read, so this is not a verdict on every formulation he has given.

The bad-argument objection and its answers are already published. Loeb 2006 endorses Millican's objection; Qu discusses the positive argument; Cottrell's natural-teleology approach supplies a further mixed causal/evaluative alternative. Their selective inspection prevents a claim of novelty or final victory over those alternatives.

**v1:** bad arguments immediately refute the causal account. **Withdrawn.**
**v2:** the reflective case excludes universal dependence on custom. **Withdrawn:** T1.3.8.14 reinstates custom.
**v3:** later imagination passages collapse Garrett's two boundaries. **Weakened after full later reply:** the stable customary transition gives positive grounds to separate them.
**Final:** favor Garrett's dependency account and separation of boundaries; retain pressure concerning faulty demonstration and the wording of T1.4.7.7. This is support for a consequential part of an existing position, not endorsement of its whole epistemology.

The most useful continuation is a complete comparison with Cottrell's causal-and-evaluative account, followed by Garrett's original book treatment. A mere repetition of the familiar bad-argument objection is closed unless new evidence changes its force.
