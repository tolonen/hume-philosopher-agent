## Initial competing models and consequences

**N — Naturalistic reflective endorsement.** Ordinary first-order beliefs and corrections need not wait for a faculty-independent vindication. A reasoner can reflect on her fallibility, compare beliefs, and adopt stable, coherent methods on the basis of experience and her intellectual dispositions. Treatise scepticism defeats an unrestricted demand for self-vindication, not every possible epistemic evaluation. This is a candidate reconstruction to compare with Garrett's actual original argument; it is not yet a complete attribution of his position.

Premises: N1, no circularity-free global vindication is available; N2, particular judgments can precede faculty assessment; N3, ordinary first-order comparison retains authority for a reflectively aware inquirer; N4, that authority is epistemic and not exhausted by the practical benefit of inquiry. N3–N4 are the disputed bridges. Consequence N-C1: the concluding Treatise should permit some informed preference among reasoning practices after encountering the regress, rather than only forgetting it. N-C2: later testimony and religious arguments should justify asymmetric evidential treatment through features of the evidence, not mere strength of inclination. Defeater: the text explicitly confines every post-sceptical acceptance to assent lacking reflective authority, or gives no grounds for N4 beyond our chosen definition.

**P — Pyrrhonian assent without active endorsement.** The sceptical regress defeats reasoning's claim to justify itself; nature nevertheless determines belief and practical participation. Strong belief, carefully argued propositions, and normative vocabulary can all occur without active rational endorsement. Causal rules can guide practice while their ultimate basis is practical. This is the parent's source-grounded report of the core Baxter challenge, to be checked against the full original in this stage.

Premises: P1, unrestricted reflective justification defeats itself; P2, passive assent can be strong, stable, theoretically articulate, and normatively worded; P3, those features therefore do not alone establish active endorsement; P4, Hume's positive philosophical practice after the crisis is entirely consistent with P2 rather than an exception to P1. P4 is the interpretive commitment needing its own evidence. Consequence P-C1: post-crisis assertions of truth or reasonableness need not refute this reading. P-C2: a mature restriction of inquiry can still govern passive practice. Defeater: Hume expressly reinstates, under awareness of the sceptical grounds, the kind of rational endorsement that this reading excludes; locating an unqualified scientific sentence will not suffice.

**D — A developmental change in the function of scepticism.** The 1739 Treatise may leave reflective authority unstable, while the Enquiry reconstructs scepticism as a durable correction of inquiry's extent and dogmatism. This could change the argument's conclusion without changing custom's psychological basis. It is a developmental hypothesis, not a licence to read the later text into the earlier one.

Premises: D1, the Treatise's unrestricted reflective process ends in an unresolved conflict; D2, the later work distinguishes excessive from mitigated scepticism and assigns the latter a positive, stable role; D3, that distinction changes the authority or structure of inquiry rather than merely its presentation. D3 is the contested bridge. Consequence D-C1: the Enquiry should describe the effect of sceptical reflection as a retained intellectual discipline, not only transient disbelief followed by distraction. D-C2: if testimony and religious inference exemplify that discipline, they should show how corrective reflection changes permitted conclusions. Defeater: the earlier Treatise already contains the same durable limitation with the same rationale, or the later text merely restates the instability while offering no new authority. Omission of the probability regress would not independently establish withdrawal.

**HPA bridge under challenge — familiar kinds delimit causal warrant.** HPA-006 proposed that prior experience of a relevant kind permits additions unavailable for an unfamiliar ultimate cause. This does not yet choose N, P, or D. Consequence K-C1: testimony may justify an unprecedented event through a familiar testimonial relation; requiring prior observation of the complete target event would be too strong. K-C2: theological inference should fail for an identified defect in the actual comparison or inferential supplement, not simply because it is theological. K-C3: if the same standards suffice in both domains, categorical rejection of all religious conclusions would need a further premise. These tests may defeat an overgeneralized restriction rather than merely produce exceptions.

## Test results: the Treatise reconstructed first

The complete sequence of T 1.4.1 matters more than any isolated normative expression. First, experience of errors requires the reasoner to assess her application of a rule (paragraphs 1–5). Hume does not initially demand a proof from outside experience: this is ordinary correction, and paragraph 2 even describes repeated checking and agreement as increasing confidence. Next, paragraphs 5–6 extend the requirement to every assessment of probability, including assessments of the assessor. Each further assessment supposedly reduces assurance, without a legitimate stopping point. Paragraphs 7–11 explain why actual belief survives: belief depends on a felt manner of conception, and remote iterations lose psychological force. Paragraph 12 expressly rejects a quick self-refutation reply. The sceptical argument also loses force; its defeat does not establish the truth of the original judgment by an independent argument.

This establishes a constraint on N: it cannot cite the practical survival of belief as if T 1.4.1 had supplied a rational stopping rule. It also constrains P: the passage is an argument about a particular account of assessment and assent, not an explicit proposition that every conceivable epistemic standard is illegitimate. Both readings must explain the transition from the process described to the normative conclusion attributed. There is a further possible fault in the regress: indefinitely many reductions need not mathematically reach zero. I do not promote that familiar-looking objection as a new discovery or as the interpretive solution. Even if the argument is invalid by our lights, Hume's treatment of its force still requires explanation. Nor does the confidence-increasing check in paragraph 2 automatically contradict paragraph 6: adding fresh first-order confirming evidence and estimating the reliability of a judgment are distinguishable operations.

T 1.4.7 makes a simple appeal to stable methods inadequate. Paragraph 3 joins memory, senses, and causal understanding through the enlivening activity of imagination. Paragraph 4 identifies a conflict between regular causal reasoning and continued matter, and treats alternately accepting the contradictory results as a difficulty. Paragraphs 6–7 reject both unrestricted fancy and a policy of accepting only established operations: the latter policy inherits the regress. A universal ban on refined thought would defeat itself and eliminate science. The text therefore does not simply select the general rules after diagnosing irregular imagination.

The remainder is nevertheless more than a temporary return to backgammon. Paragraphs 11–14 explain renewed philosophical motivation, compare philosophy with superstition, recommend attention to experience, anticipate critical examination, and identify a limited field in which conviction is possible. Paragraph 14 also applies diffidence to philosophical doubts. These are constraints on an unrestricted P claim that the conclusion does nothing except forget scepticism. But paragraph 15 explicitly qualifies the confident expressions used in the ensuing work. Confident scientific language is therefore weak evidence, by itself, for N's disputed reflective authority. The closing trajectory supports continued discriminating inquiry; it does not unambiguously decide the philosophical standing of every discrimination.

**Result for the recorded predictions:** N-C1 survives as a claim about informed preference, but N3–N4 do not follow from its survival. P-C1 survives; P4 is not established by detecting involuntary assent. The strong version of D-C1—durable limitation first appears in the Enquiry—is withdrawn. T 1.4.7.13–14 is direct adverse evidence against that version. The later work may make the discipline more explicit and accessible, without thereby first inventing it or vindicating it.

## Strongest original scholarly alternatives

**Baxter, directly read in full.** Donald L. M. Baxter, “A Pyrrhonian Interpretation of Hume on Assent,” published in *Skepticism: From Antiquity to the Present* (2018), 380–394; accessed the complete 26-page author manuscript, including its earlier forthcoming-2016 header. Manuscript pages 7–11 distinguish an attitude from the proposition entertained; 14–18 treat the regress as epistemically normative and causal rules as ultimately practical; 19–22 explain strong, theoretical, and normatively articulated passive assent, including the later Enquiry. This assessment now has direct original coverage rather than relying on the parent's report. [Author manuscript](https://philpapers.org/archive/BAXAIS.pdf).

The strongest defence of P is that the truth-seeking instructions at the start of T 1.4.1 belong to the same process that destroys assurance at its end. Merely repeating Hume's subsequent judgments of truth or using an epistemic “ought” cannot answer this objection; the attitude taken toward those judgments is precisely in dispute. The most vulnerable step, however, is an inference from the causal and involuntary character of belief to the absence of normatively adequate belief. A belief can be caused by a considered comparison of evidence; inability to choose its conclusion at will does not entail that it lacks an adequate reason. P needs its further premise about the regress, not only a causal theory of assent. Conversely, an N response that calls all surviving belief “endorsement” without answering that premise simply redescribes the disputed outcome. This is a live disagreement about the status of the reflective process, not a terminological victory for either side.

**Garrett, a complete reply assessed as its own argument.** Don Garrett, “Loeb's ‘Standard’ Questions about Hume's Concept of Probable Truth,” *Hume Studies* 40(2) (2014), 279–300: all 23 PDF pages, including cover, notes, and references, directly read. His proposal develops probability through feeling and correction; truth evaluations precede faculty evaluations. Semantic ascent brings first-order beliefs into competition with sceptical judgments about faculties (285–289), without an a priori balancing rule. A shared standard permits disagreements and possible global error (283, 290–292). His body-belief defence incorporates noninferential deliverances of sense while distinguishing a defective sub-operation from the complete faculty (293–296). [Original reply](https://humesociety.org/?hume_article_pdf=16385).

This is a serious answer to a foundation-demand objection. If particular judgments already possess defeasible epistemic standing, the demand that they first prove their source reliable reverses the order of evaluation. A standard can be constitutive of correct probability judgments without being a further premise in every such judgment. The objections below therefore do **not** infer that naturalistic epistemic evaluation is impossible simply because no independent faculty proof is available.

The pressure point is more specific. When the analysis of a belief discloses a false identification, it is necessary to show which content retains its initial authority after that defeater. Passing from a lively belief to a belief in its truth adds a level of representation; it does not independently discriminate the true component from the false component. For continued bodies, the question is whether a general existential commitment can survive the criticism of identifying intermittent perceptions with continued things, and whether that surviving content is supported by the standard or merely placed within it. That cannot be settled by the general legitimacy of first-order correction. The original reply gives an answer—sense as a composite faculty—but its application to the Treatise's explicit conflict needs argument. This is a targeted burden, not a refutation of the sense-based account.

A further asymmetry must be retained. The standard may reasonably include a complex capacity although one operation within it is error-prone; ordinary perception already requires such discrimination. Thus identifying an unreliable sub-operation does not automatically discredit every product of that capacity. A successful objection must show that the *particular contested content* depends on the defective operation, and that the proposed correction supplies no new basis for it. Otherwise the objection is a genetic fallacy. The parent has reread the full T 1.4.2 sequence to test precisely this dependency.

**Qu and the novelty/development limit.** Hsueh Qu, “Hume's Regulative Epistemology in the Enquiry,” *Canadian Journal of Philosophy* 54(3), 207–218 (issue dated 2024; article copyright/DOI and online publication 2025), read in full with notes and references. Qu argues for increased actionable guidance and a broader audience in the Enquiry, distinguishing that claim from his earlier thesis of substantive epistemological evolution. He considers shared doctrines, the Treatise's causal rules, and difficulty applying the later advice. The argument at 212 n.21 also recognizes the difference between antecedent doubt and the Treatise's consequent scepticism. [Original open-access article](https://doi.org/10.1017/can.2025.8).

This blocks a novelty claim for D understood as increased practical regulation. It does not establish a first appearance of epistemic authority. In particular, E 12.3 rejects universal doubt undertaken before inquiry, whereas T 1.4.1 generates doubt through experience of error during inquiry. Rejecting the former does not logically refute the latter. E 12.22–25 must do additional work if a substantive withdrawal is alleged. A more explicit instructional presentation can be a consequential literary and practical change while leaving the normative dispute unresolved. The evidence considered here does not date such a change more precisely than the witnesses permit.

## Later comparison: a changed organization, not an imported cure

E 12.1–34 and its notes were read as one argument. Paragraphs 3–4 distinguish universal antecedent doubt from cautious preparation. Paragraphs 6–16 distinguish corrections of misleading appearances from the deeper difficulty of explaining or justifying the external source of perception. Thus ordinary correction and the deeper problem are already separated inside this later text. Paragraphs 17–20 present difficulties in abstract reasoning and doubts about the sceptical conclusion itself. Paragraphs 21–23 distinguish weak popular attacks from deeper arguments concerning custom; ordinary action defeats a complete practical suspension without furnishing the desired ultimate account. Paragraphs 24–26 make the useful residue explicit: continuing caution, reduced dogmatism, and bounds on inquiry. Paragraphs 27–34 map allowable inquiry, including a qualified treatment of theology in paragraph 32.

The gain for D is an explicit, teachable organization of sceptical discipline. The limit is equally explicit: paragraph 23 leaves the foundation objections unanswered, and paragraph 25 retains the absence of a satisfactory reason for the familiar inference from repeated experiments. It is therefore false to say that the later work simply replaces the early predicament with a faculty-independent warrant. Whether its positive correction embodies a constitutive epistemic standard, or a practical discipline of assent, remains the N/P disagreement. The version of D that resolves that disagreement merely by contrast of tone is rejected.

E 5.1–12 provides an additional check, read only to that extent in this stage. The criticism of philosophy that indulges a predominant passion, the special place given to love of truth, the efficacy and authority of custom, and the involuntariness of belief resist a flat story of later rationalism replacing early feeling. They also prevent treating every passion-backed philosophical conviction as equally licensed. These paragraphs do not by themselves settle how a preference for truth becomes an epistemic standard. The reference to an existing sensory or memorial starting point in E 5.7 is particularly important: a chain of individually acceptable hypothetical links is insufficient to establish actual existence without such an anchor. A coherent causal model is therefore not automatically an existentially grounded one.

## Test across testimony and religious inference

The whole of E 10.1–41, including the substantial notes, and E 11.1–30 with notes was read. This supplies three tests of K rather than treating religious examples as automatic confirmations.

1. **An unfamiliar event can be credible.** E 10.5–7 grounds testimony in experienced report/fact relations and discriminates contexts and witnesses. E 10.10 and its note distinguish absence of experience in unfamiliar conditions from experience against an event under matching conditions. E 10.36 expressly permits acceptance of unprecedented worldwide darkness on extensive uniform testimony and relevant analogies. Any K rule requiring prior experience of the complete target event fails these cases. What matters is the evidential relation and the applicability of the comparison, not merely membership of the target in a familiar event-list.

2. **Confidence and apparent sincerity are inadequate controls.** E 10.16–19 gives a causal explanation of why a person's belief and report can become more forceful while less trustworthy; 10.25–29 and the Abbé Paris note make the problem acute even where some ordinary credentials and counter-investigation are present. These passages are important for our own method: one may not treat a stable disposition, interpersonal agreement, or the surviving force of a belief as sufficient evidence of epistemic quality. A naturalistic standard must discriminate the mechanism and circumstances. This does not itself refute Garrett, whose standard includes exclusions; it defeats an impoverished version of N based on stability alone.

3. **The content of the inferred cause determines the permitted return inference.** E 11's friend initially grants a design inference for purposes of argument, then rejects additional predictions not licensed by the evidence from which the cause was inferred. The narrator's unfinished-building and footprint objections force the qualification: other experience supplies what the single effect cannot. The narrator finally challenges the premise of a wholly unparalleled cause/effect inference itself (11.30). The dialogue therefore yields a conditional restriction with an explicit challenge and reply, not an unqualified declaration in one authorial voice. The same evidential defect applies to a wholly unknown material cause as to a wholly unknown intelligent cause.

The E 10 counterexample revises K substantively: admissibility depends on *which relation licenses which added proposition*. It does not depend simply on whether the conclusion is familiar, material, mental, ordinary, or religious. The revision changes a decision: a new event supported by independently well-attested report relations remains assessable; an apparent explanation that imports further causal capacities solely to derive its desired prediction remains unsupported. However, the particular independence of the extra evidence must be argued. Relabeling information as “kind knowledge” cannot ensure that it was not itself borrowed from the target explanation.

There is also a severe limit to promotion. E 10's acceptance of some extraordinary testimony and E 12.32's qualified admission of theology mean that a general religious/scientific partition is not supplied by K. The parent's affirmative passages from the *Natural History of Religion* and moral Enquiry must remain adverse evidence against that stronger partition. This assessor has received that report but has not reread those complete works in v5.

## Consequential revision of the integrated model

The emerging model should expose two distinct dependencies:

- **Within-practice comparison:** given a field of objects, remembered episodes, and reported events, compare candidate accounts by the experienced relations relevant to the particular proposition. Discriminate the target of the report, its production, its transmitter, and any proposed addition. Hume's first-order causal rules and testimonial reasoning make such comparisons possible.
- **Constitution of that field:** explain how the episodes count as repetitions of a cause, the perceptions as appearances of one continued object, and a sequence of acts as the history of one person. The mechanism making those comparisons available can include imaginative completion and identity attribution. Success at the first level does not itself prove the content supplied at the second.

This is an inferential distinction, not a proposal for two sealed psychological faculties. The same operation can contribute to both. Nor does it require that human beings actually entertain a conditional hypothesis before ordinary belief. It is an audit of what a reconstruction has established, not an attribution of a hidden formal calculus to Hume.

Consider the parent's porter-and-letter case in T 1.4.2.20–22. A regular transition across an interruption can be used in ordinary reasoning, while the explanation of belief in uninterrupted objects introduces an operation beyond direct custom. Reproducing the prediction therefore does not independently justify the extra continuity content. Consider also a personal-history claim: finding reliable relations among passions and behaviour can support another mental episode without deciding whether all the episodes constitute one strictly identical subject. Increasing the predictive accuracy of a character description does not solve that individuation question. The same cross-person causal connectedness that enables sympathy prevents “causally connected” from being a sufficient criterion of one person.

This distinction **changes the model's conclusions**, not just its wording. The old route “local causal correction succeeds, therefore we have established the epistemic legitimacy of the objects/persons needed for the science” is rejected. The repaired route can defend particular additions and corrections while withholding that further result. But the model must not escape testing by calling every inconvenient presupposition a separate layer. For each case it must specify (a) the content relied on, (b) the operation supplying it, (c) the particular conclusion licensed, and (d) the actual defeater. When all four remain unspecified, “two layers” is only a label.

A rigorous positive application would distinguish three judgments about the same document: that it records an event; that its author believed an event; and that a political or religious conception was available to its author. Evidence against the first can coexist with evidence for the third without contradiction because the inferred facts and the relevant generating relations differ. The parent's Xenophon example appears suited to that test, but its essay text and variants were read by the parent, not independently here. The usefulness of this distinction is real; its originality remains unestablished, and no familiar historical source criticism should be advertised as an AI discovery.

## Final adversarial verdict and chronological limits

**Particular inference failure:** neither successful local correction nor causal connectedness by itself supplies the needed ontological or personal-identity conclusion. K's event-level familiarity version is defeated by the testimony cases. These are genuine revisions of the prior integrated proposal.

**Interpretation weakened:** a blanket early/later contrast between unstable recovery and first introduction of durable discipline fails against T 1.4.7.13–14. A later change in organization and intended practical reach is supported and already substantially argued in the literature. Its becoming a new epistemic foundation is unproved.

**Unresolved:** N and P remain substantial competitors after the full original readings available here. The former supplies a constitutive standard and a mechanism for weighing first-order against higher-order judgments; the latter makes the normative force of the regress central and distinguishes the attitude of assent from its content. Neither is refuted by the mere existence of Hume's positive science. The two-layer revision constrains a route from that science to metaphysical conclusions but does not by itself adjudicate this deeper dispute. It should not be promoted as doing so.

The reconstruction of Book 1 pertains to the 1739 argument as represented in the edited HTO text; no claim is made that a 1740 Appendix formulation appeared in 1739. Baxter's appeal to Appendix 2 must be kept chronologically separate from T 1.4.1.8. The personal-identity reservation in the Appendix was read in v4 and remains a later qualification, not a premise silently inserted into the original closing chapter. HTO's Enquiry witness is the edited 1777 state. This assessment has not collated the relevant formulations against 1748 or dated each revision, so it cannot claim that a particular contrast was already present in precisely this wording in 1748. Likewise, this stage supplies no evidence about composition dates beyond the textual/publication distinctions already established by the project. A chronological model must preserve these limits even when the later passage appears interpretively convenient.
