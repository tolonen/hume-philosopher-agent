## Revised question and attribution bounds

Can Hume's causal inquiry discriminate episodes and assign them to persons without deciding whether a complex is a further single being? If so, does that suffice to explain the union of perceptions in consciousness, or only leave a distinct explanatory dependency unresolved?

Direct complete-source verification now strengthens B's attribution: [Baxter, *Hume on Substance: A Critique of Locke*](https://philarchive.org/archive/BAXHOS-4), penultimate manuscript dated 6 January 2014 for the 2015 Lodge/Stoneham volume, all 27 pages and notes read. Its pp. 5–10 defend a collective reading even of real compound ideas; pp. 17–23 distinguish several kinds of separability; pp. 25–27 answer Hakkarainen 2012's partial-distinction repair and deny asymmetric dependence between strictly existing relata. This confirms the initial conjunction and identifies its crucial restricted domain. No new scholarly words are quoted.

H is based on the complete 2012 article and complete 2015 spatial-properties manuscript already read, not a proxy for the 2019 unity paper. The restricted separability repair is an interpretation of what Hume could consistently mean, not proof that he consciously recognized a third distinction. The spatial account is expressly narrower than a universal ontology of minds. **The 2019 original remains unassessed.** This stage retried the institutional link, PhilArchive, exact-title/DOI searches, and current and earlier publisher routes. No legitimate complete copy was obtained. Earlier author-profile searches also failed. Neither an abstract nor Baxter's reply to the 2012 article establishes the later argument's premises.

The common ground should be stated narrowly. Both accessible accounts permit real constituents, their organization, and descriptions involving them; B explicitly permits collective discourse. They do not share an established theorem that empirical warrant is independent of composition. Requiring discriminable causal terms is a semantic/explanatory presupposition, not yet a theory of metaphysical individuation. The inference from that presupposition to a numerically single composite is false without another premise. Conversely, failure of that inference does not prove that every person-directed attribution has all its individuative premises in order. The shared explanatory ground and the disputed ontological conclusion must therefore remain separate.

## The earlier argument reconstructed in its 1739 setting

The spatial argument begins with a finite perceptual capacity, adequate ideas of minimal extension, the representation of extension by arranged colored or tangible constituents, and a conceivability-to-possibility transition. In [T1.2.2](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/2/2), the Malezieu passage supports actual indivisible units against infinite divisibility. [T1.2.3](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/2/3) then makes actual compound extension depend on actual constituents. The disputed inference is from those commitments to a further single whole. The argument's physical and psychological roles must not be reduced to casual singular grammar.

In [T1.4.3](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/3), Hume explains our attribution of simplicity to connected qualities and our invention of a substance to sustain it. This does not merely suspend judgment about the nature of a securely admitted bearer: .7 rejects the supposed dependence on that unintelligible substance. An explanation without such a bearer is one result; its adequacy as a complete ontology is another.

[T1.4.5](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/5) separately permits the causal comparison of bodily dispositions and thoughts. Its .29–33 argument uses experienced conjunction rather than an intelligible transmission of thought through a substance. The cause/effect relata can differ in kind. Crucially, .9–16 precludes solving every mental union by spatial arrangement: some perceptions have spatial parts, while passions, sounds and tastes cannot acquire dimensions by accumulation. Causal relations therefore cannot simply inherit the criterion of spatial composition.

The personal-identity argument then conjoins: separable perceptions; lack of an impression of an invariable simple self; the difference between strict identity and a related succession; and resemblance/causation generating an easy reflective transition. [T1.4.6.16–20](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/6) treats this as explaining personal identity, including extensions beyond remembered episodes. Its causal republic analogy allows character change. The unsupported bridge for a complete reduction is that an explanation of transition among ideas also explains why **this** train constitutes one consciousness. The text starts with a person's train; it does not first derive a boundary from the entire causal network.

This is a criticism of that stronger reduction, not proof that Hume lacked every ordinary way to distinguish people.

## Formal correspondence and results

Let `E(x,s)` mean that item x exists in scenario s, `p < w` mean that p is a proper constituent of the exact whole w, `D(x,y)` mean numerical nonidentity, and `RD(x,y)` mean Hakkarainen2012's stronger real distinction, requiring no common parts. This excludes whole/part overlap and is stronger than mere mutual noninclusion. `MS(x,y)` means that one can be clearly conceived without the other; `Poss` is possibility, not belief or knowledge. `HumeAccepts(P)` is a historical attribution, not the truth of P.

The smallest disputed conjunction in the spatial repair is:

1. A real whole w and proper constituent p are numerically nonidentical.
2. `Nec(E(w) -> E(p))`: that exact whole cannot exist without its essential constituent.
3. For any numerically nonidentical real relata x,y, `Poss(E(x) and not E(y))`.

With x=w and y=p, 2 rules out the scenario demanded by 3. H restricts 3 to RD; B denies that w is an additional strictly existing relatum. Those are different repairs. Leibniz's Law establishes w≠p if their properties differ; it does not by itself establish RD or the separability conclusion. Conversely, the formal consistency of H's repair does not show that Hume accepts its scope restriction. The dispute is not settled by calling the whole a system.

A dependency-free enumeration of all sixteen two-scenario valuations for w and p returned nine frames satisfying 2, seven satisfying the modal witness required by 3, and zero satisfying both. The impossibility follows directly from the formulas and is not limited to this finite enumeration. The computation checks our translation, not the textual premise assignments.

Now let `C(a,b)` represent an empirically attributed causal relation among episode tokens; `Owner(a,k)` assigns episode a to personal history k; `Whole(P,k)` says the assigned episodes constitute a further single being. On four tokens with fixed chain edges a→b→c→d, owner assignments `{a,b}/{c,d}` and `{a}/{b,c,d}` leave every causal edge unchanged. There are seven nontrivial two-history partitions of the same four-token graph. Thus the graph alone does not determine Owner. Changing which history owns an episode changes where a cause becomes interpersonal without changing the bare causal pattern. Adding further bodily, memory or first-person evidence can discriminate assignments; the example claims only that C alone cannot.

Nor does an Owner assignment entail Whole: a plural quantification over the assigned tokens can preserve their causal relations without adding a composite to the domain. That logical alternative supports C2, but does not establish that Hume endorses the plural translation or that real composite causation is impossible. No countermodel is treated as a scholarly refutation.

Appendix21's two listed principles — distinct perceptions are distinct existences; the mind perceives no real connection among distinct existences — are not a formal contradiction by themselves. A model of independent perceptions and no perceived binding satisfies both. A contradiction would require a further demand about a unified consciousness and a bridge specifying what could explain it. Appendix20 states the explanatory demand; Appendix21 offers a simple bearer or perceived real connection as ways out, without explicitly proving these exhaust all possible explanations. Therefore our formal diagnosis is **a missing explanatory bridge**, not a proof that Hume's two sentences are logically inconsistent. In particular, no perceived real connection must not be translated as no actual connection.

## Development: what changed and what remained

The 1740 [Appendix](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/app) is an explicit admission that the present explanation of personal identity is defective, not mere later omission. It retains the case against an independently perceived simple self and the distinctness/separability of perceptions, including the compositional description at .15. It does not identify a replacement theory. Consequently, the earlier confidence that the associative explanation completes the intellectual account must be qualified, while its constituent-level claims are not expressly withdrawn.

At the same stage, Appendix2–9 retains and clarifies the explanation of causal belief; .4 uses inference from an acquaintance's voice and partially visible body. This creates a discriminating developmental result: the local belief explanation remains asserted while the constitutive explanation fails. It does not demonstrate that the former is epistemically independent of every assumption the latter leaves ungrounded. Book3's T3.3.1.4–7, published with the Appendix in 1740, continues person-directed character attribution and sympathy. Publication together documents coexistence; the composition order and a deliberate post-retraction repair are not established here.

The edited website relocates original Appendix interventions into Book1. This note separates the expressly targeted personal-identity qualification from inserted illustrations and the two smaller corrections at Appendix22. Its distance/angle correction and force/vivacity qualification are not a general repudiation of spatial composition or causal reasoning. No original-print facsimile or manuscript composition chronology was collated in this assessment.

## Strongest surviving reconstruction, objection, and consequential revision

The positive result is a two-premise explanatory structure rather than a blanket exemption of science from ontology. **Local causal inference uses discriminable episode kinds; person-directed inference additionally uses an assignment of signs, actions and episodes to personal histories.** Neither assignment logically entails a numerically single complex substance. But the absence of that entailment does not make the assignment dispensable or prove its warrant.

Our initial C4 demanded an independent basis for assignment too broadly. Empirical tracking can use causal and noncausal evidence together. The necessary correction is C4*: do not establish the assignment solely by the very stable-character regularity subsequently presented as its confirmation. Jointly revised body trajectories, contextual histories and episode patterns might constrain one another. This is a proposal for a substantive theory; it is not a claim that Hume completed it or a foundational proof of other minds.

The concrete consequence is that **T1.4.5's cause-versus-substance separation does not by itself justify HPA-006's person-specific causal inferences**. A world might preserve that bodily signs of a certain kind indicate pain while leaving which continuing history owns the inferred episode underdetermined. Different assignments yield different character estimates and moral attributions despite the same local cause/effect relations. T2.1.11.16–17 makes that difference explanatory: combining kinship and proximity in the same people yields a different result from spreading them across different people. Merely predicting an emotion somewhere does not explain this contrast.

The strongest objection is that ordinary person tracking already gives all the individuation needed; demanding a metaphysical foundation wrongly revives the substantial self. The response is to distinguish a demand for a simple bearer from a demand to specify the assignment the explanation actually uses. Neither H nor B excludes ordinary tracking. Its success can support an empirical assignment, but cannot alone establish a real composite, strict persistence, or the unity of consciousness. This does not defeat all HPA-006 inferences. It defeats the claim that their evidential premises have already been fully exposed by the own-case analogy and ignorance of a bearer.

Another sharp difficulty remains within 1739: T2.1.11.4 makes the lively conception of ourselves pivotal to sympathy, whereas T1.4.6.2 denies an impression of an invariable simple self. A familiar collection may explain the former without admitting the latter; that interpretation is plausible but does not solve the Appendix's unity demand. Treating the two claims as automatically harmonious would protect our model with an unargued premise.

The resulting philosophical contribution is a consequential revision of our account, not an advertised reconciliation: preserve local causal explanation, expose and test its person-assignment dependency, and keep the constitutive question open. Hakkarainen and Baxter contest what organized constituents are; their disagreement does not establish whether a particular attribution is warranted. The Appendix in turn blocks treating that separation of disputes as a completed theory of one consciousness.
