# Unity, identity, and the limits of causal person-tracking

12 October 2026. Nonblinded automated adversarial assessment, inherited parent model without override. Exact model identifier and sampling configuration are not exposed. Scope: test a proposed integration with HPA-006 against the complete primary arguments in T 1.4.6, the Appendix's personal-identity discussion, and T 1.4.2. No verdict on inaccessible secondary originals is made.

**Verdict:** the distinction among unity at a time, strict identity through time, and causal coherence is textually well grounded. It supports a conditional compatibility argument, not a completed reconciliation of Hakkarainen and Baxter. The stronger proposal that causal organization solves personal unity must be withheld: the Appendix expressly leaves the problem unresolved. HPA-006 can retain local causal attribution, but its use of stable character needs a stated dependence on empirical person-tracking.

## Four claims that must not be exchanged

| Claim | Primary basis | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One object is considered at a time | T 1.4.2.26, SBN 200: “One single object conveys the idea of unity, not that of identity.” | That the object is indivisible, simple, fundamental, or identical with an object at another time. |
| An object is strictly identical through time | T 1.4.2.29–30, SBN 200–201; T 1.4.6.6, SBN 253–255: invariableness and uninterruptedness through a supposed variation of time. | That any causally continuous or similarly functioning succession satisfies this condition. |
| Parts or successive events are causally related | T 1.4.6.12, .19, SBN 257, 261. | That they lose their distinctness, form an indivisible subject, or meet strict identity's condition. |
| A complex is treated as perfectly simple | T 1.4.6.22, SBN 263: close relations facilitate a transition resembling the consideration of something indivisible. | That every related complex is unreal, or that having several parts is itself a contradiction. |

The first item concerns Hume's account of the idea of unity. It should not be converted without argument into a contemporary theory of mereological composition or a denial of logical self-identity. Likewise, Hume's distinction between unity and identity concerns the derivation and temporal use of these ideas, not a demonstration that the logical formula `a = a` is false.

The context matters. T 1.4.2.29 invokes a fiction in applying duration to an unchanging object. That contribution to the formation of the idea is not identical to the error of ascribing strict identity to a changing succession. T 1.4.2.31 explicitly marks the subsequent accommodation to the vulgar identification of objects and perceptions; .38–40 explains how a supposition is formed and rendered conceivable; .43–47 criticizes its philosophical standing. The passages cannot all be read as unqualified endorsements of one ontology. [Complete T 1.4.2](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/2).

## What the mass, ship, and republic establish

T 1.4.6.8, SBN 255–256, is an important constraint. A supposed contiguous, connected mass has perfect identity if its parts remain uninterruptedly and invariably the same, despite motion or relocation of the whole or its parts. Adding or subtracting even a small part destroys strict identity, though ordinary judgment often overlooks this. Thus the simplistic inference **complexity implies lack of identity**, or **any change whatsoever destroys identity**, is not Hume's expressed criterion.

The case is conditional: Hume asks us to suppose such a mass. It does not establish that an actual composite meeting all the conditions persists in nature. A comprehensive composition nihilist might also seek to interpret this as talk within a supposition rather than an assertion of a further composite entity. That reply requires a fuller argument, but the conditional example alone does not refute it. The secure result is that denying strict identity to changing things is insufficient to infer a denial of composition as such.

T 1.4.6.11–14 then explains why purpose, reciprocal causal dependence, and expected change sustain ordinary identity attributions to ships, organisms, churches, and rivers. These are not newly discovered exceptions that silently repeal the strict criterion. Hume has already distinguished that criterion from the ordinary transition through related objects. The rebuilt church, in .13, can receive the same name without linguistic impropriety even when its form and materials differ. But .7 and .21 also warn that the issue exceeds words when the attribution brings an imagined unchanging principle with it. Hence neither “all identity talk is false and useless” nor “all identity disputes are merely verbal” accurately states the complete argument.

T 1.4.6.19, SBN 261, provides the republic comparison: a system of perceptions can retain causal connections through changes of members and character. Read it with .15–16, which classifies personal identity as fictitious and keeps each perception distinct, and .21, which denies a sharp standard for when connected objects deserve the name of identity. The republic does not secretly restore a strictly identical substance. It supplies an account of organization and the associated attribution.

T 1.4.6.22 is about the further attribution of **simplicity** to coexistent parts already described as closely related. Denial of perfect simplicity does not by itself deny the parts, their relations, or every legitimate way of considering them together. Equally, the paragraph does not settle what makes an aggregate a real one rather than many: that metaphysical addition would need independent support. [Complete T 1.4.6 and its note](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/6).

## Conditional integration, without attributing unread positions

Suppose one interpretation establishes spatial organization or phenomenal unity at a time, while another denies strict temporal identity to changing complexes. Those claims can coexist: one organized complex now does not guarantee that a later arrangement is strictly the same complex. This is the permitted integration hypothesis supplied for this assessment.

But three stronger transitions remain invalid without additional premises:

1. From spatial or phenomenal unity to a simple, indivisible subject.
2. From the failure of strict temporal identity to the nonexistence of every composite.
3. From physical spatial organization to a sufficient account of mental unity.

The third is especially important. T 1.4.6.17 expressly drops contiguity from the relevant account of personal identity, concentrating on resemblance and causation. A treatment of spatial wholes cannot simply be transferred to successive perceptions. Nor can this primary-text comparison establish that particular published Hakkarainen and Baxter arguments actually have these limited scopes. Their exact relevant originals must be read before asserting agreement, contradiction, or priority. This note supplies discriminants for that reading rather than adjudicating unavailable claims.

## The Appendix is an objection to an easy solution

The 1740 Appendix must not be folded into the confident 1739 exposition without marking the qualification. App.10 announces the difficulty. App.11–19 retains the arguments against a simple substantial self and the distinctness of perceptions. App.15 even describes the self in terms of their composition; this makes a blanket denial of every form of composition particularly unsafe. Yet App.20–21 says the account of connection and the uniting of successive perceptions in thought or consciousness is unsatisfactory.

Two distinctions preserve what the Appendix actually concedes. First, Hume does not renounce both the distinctness of perceptions and the absence of perceived real connection: he explicitly cannot abandon either. Second, the conclusion is neither a completed solution nor a declaration of necessary insolubility. The difficulty defeats his present explanation; he leaves possible future reconciliation open. [Appendix, especially App.10–21, SBN 633–636](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/app).

This is the strongest obstacle to integrating HPA-006 into a comprehensive theory of the person. Local causal explanations may remain available without answering substance questions. But one cannot infer from that availability that Hume has explained why precisely these experiences compose one consciousness. App.20 contains the very causal/associative resources to which such a quick solution would appeal and still reports failure.

There is also a structural problem for our reconstruction: **causal connectedness is not sufficient to individuate a mind**. Sympathy and ordinary interaction connect different persons causally. A rule that groups every causally connected experience into one person would erase that distinction. Restricting the rule to an appropriate kind or history of dependence may help, but the restriction itself requires an account. Adding the word “system” cannot supply that account by itself. This is our adversarial argument, not an attribution of a particular diagnosis to Hume or to either scholar.

## Stable character: permissible evidence, insufficient foundation

HPA-006 uses surrounding circumstances, later conduct, and stable character to constrain other-minds attribution. The last item is legitimate only with an important qualification. T 3.3.1.4–5, SBN 575, treats actions as signs of durable principles and makes character, rather than an isolated action, central to moral assessment. But these paragraphs already concern conduct belonging to a person; they do not establish the numerical or substantial identity of that person. T 3.3.1.7, SBN 575–576, supplies the inference from a person's signs to a passion without immediate access to that passion. [T 3.3.1.4–7](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/3/3/1).

The sharpest check is T 1.4.6.19 itself: the person may change character and disposition without losing the identity being discussed. Stable character is therefore **not necessary** for that identity. Resembling character in two people is also not sufficient to make them one person. A method that groups acts by the very trait it then claims to confirm risks circularity.

A defensible reconstruction has two levels. Within ordinary inquiry, bodily trajectories, causal histories, memories, testimony, and contextual continuity can supply defeasible ways of tracking whom an episode concerns. Character evidence can then confirm or undermine an attributed disposition within that history. This need not assert an unchanging soul or perfect identity. However, calling these practices defeasible does not vindicate them against every sceptical challenge or solve their ontological foundation. In particular, testimony does not establish other minds without presuppositions about speakers, and the Appendix's account of unified consciousness remains unresolved.

Safe wording for integration is: **“Character and subsequent conduct constrain an attribution within an empirically tracked personal history. They do not independently establish strict identity, and Hume's account of what unites the perceptions remains explicitly unsettled in the Appendix.”**

## Explicit inference and bounded conclusion

**Textual premises:** T 1.4.2.26–30 distinguishes the idea of unity from temporal identity; T 1.4.6.6–14 distinguishes strict identity from the ordinary attribution sustained by relations; .19 applies causal organization to a person while allowing character change; .22 separately addresses simplicity; App.20–21 leaves the principle uniting successive perceptions unexplained.

**Interpretive premise:** empirical attribution may employ patterns of continuity without assuming strict identity or a simple bearer. This is supported by the positive descriptions and ordinary uses in the same text, but it is not a completed reduction of persons to those patterns.

**Inference:** denial of strict identity or perfect simplicity does not automatically erase every relational description needed by empirical inquiry. Conversely, the availability of such descriptions does not settle their constitution as one mind or one enduring thing.

**Conclusion:** HPA-006 survives as an account of local causal commitment and its limited additions. It should not be expanded into a demonstrated solution of composition, personal unity, or diachronic identity. A spatial-unity account and a strict-identity criticism may be compatible if their actual claims match these distinctions. Their published reconciliation and the novelty of this integration remain unassessed here.
