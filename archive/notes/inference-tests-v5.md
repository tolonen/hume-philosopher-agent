## Central question and competing models

Why can inference from observable conduct support unobserved mental activity while a natural-order argument fails to establish a providential designer's further attributes or effects? Does the difference concern the subject matter, the available evidence and projection, or the kind of assent with which any conclusion is accepted?

**M1: absolute empirical/theological prohibition.** Reconstructed premise: causal inference is permitted only for ordinary natural causes, and inference to a divine mind is categorically illegitimate. Additional bridge: the restriction follows from Hume's causal epistemology rather than a particular evidence deficit. Prediction: an intelligent voice unique in its scale or source cannot legitimately license any inference to intelligence if it is classified as divine. Minimal design analogy in D XII must be merely concession, rhetorical performance, or involuntary opinion, not an allowed inference. Religious testimony should be excluded because it is religious, even when the reported event has ordinary corroboration. Defeat condition: explicit acceptance of a bounded mental-type inference across the alleged categorical boundary, or a permissible extraordinary event later used in an inappropriate religious inference. M1 is a test model, not attributed wholesale to a named scholar.

**M2: evidence-calibrated analogical extension with restricted attribute projection.** Reconstructed premises: experience teaches relations between observable patterns and causal/mental types; sufficiently relevant resemblance can support an unobserved instance. Strength decreases with relevant dissimilarity and inadequate contrast cases. A conclusion projects only attributes licensed by its evidence, and extension to further effects requires independently learned information about the cause's kind. Additional bridges to test: what fixes relevant resemblance without assuming the inferred cause; whether one can infer a minimally mental type without an independently sampled divine species; whether correction by evidence is epistemic warrant or only regular practical judgment. Predictions: animal reason and sympathy allow indirect mental inferences; a unique intelligible voice can support some intelligence despite lack of repeated cosmic voices; a singular universe sharply weakens rather than automatically cancels resemblance; D XII can retain minimal analogy while refusing anthropomorphic additions; EHU XI blocks enrichment and return inference when the cause is known only through its effect. Miracle testimony is weighed comparatively rather than excluded by its subject matter. Defeat condition: a consistently categorical boundary despite adequately matched evidence, or a licensed additional attribute/effect whose only support is the proposed conclusion itself.

**M3: default empirical practice under sceptical passive assent.** Reconstructed premises: custom compels ordinary causal belief; sceptical reflection gives no ultimate truth-directed reason to endorse it; inquiry nevertheless distinguishes stable from erratic transitions and useful from dangerous beliefs. The contrast with theology arises because theological extensions lack the experiential force and practical reliability enjoyed by ordinary attribution. Additional bridges: all affirmative scientific or evaluative assertions must be compatible with passive assent; practical stability must explain Hume's selection of good testimony rather than merely redescribe that selection. Predictions: animal and human mental belief may be definite while ultimately unvindicated; D XII's minimal conclusion can be believed without active endorsement; EHU X comparative evidence and EHU XII corrigibility need not vindicate reason against foundation-level scepticism. Defeat condition: a passage that answers foundation-level scepticism with independently certified truth-conduciveness, not merely local evidence language; alternatively, an inability to explain why the same custom-produced transitions are approved in one context and rejected in another. M3 competes with M2 over ultimate authority; its motivational account can coexist with M2's content restrictions, which is itself a discriminating consequence.

## Joint-premise dependencies and prospective tests

Let `L(E,C)` mean that Hume licenses a local inference from evidence E to cause C in the argumentative context; `A(C,F)` means an independently learned relation between the inferred causal kind and further property/effect F; `U(E,C)` means the estimate of C depends entirely on E. These symbols represent interpretive attributions, not truth, knowledge, or actual metaphysical necessity.

M2 predicts `L(E,C) and U(E,C)` does not entail `L(E,F)` for a further attribute/effect. An additional bridge `A(C,F)` and an appropriate evidential comparison are required. Thus permitting an intelligent cause does not by itself permit perfection, infinite power, benevolence, future reward, or immortality. M1 predicts absence of `L(E,C)` whenever C is divine. M3 leaves the practical/local relation L in place while denying that L alone establishes ultimate rational endorsement. A countermodel to the inference from a positive causal statement to active endorsement is therefore possible without denying the statement's content.

## Results, completed 13 October 2026

**The broad positive bridge fails.** Evidence-calibrated attribution of unobserved mental activity does not provide Hume's justification of continued bodily existence. Hume explicitly distinguishes the imagination's extension of perceptual regularity from direct causal custom. The surviving result is a constraint on reconstruction: an account of legitimate local causal inference must separately explain its relation to the imagination's completion of objects and to reflective assent. A content restriction cannot answer all three questions.

Model identifiers are local to this note. For integration with REORIENTATION-v5.md: this M1 is an additional categorical-ban rival, not root M1; this M2 corresponds to root M3, calibrated inference; this M3 corresponds to a qualified version of root M2, passive assent. Root M1 is scientific foundationalism, and root M4 concerns development. The failed expansion of this M2 into a foundation for object ontology is adverse to root M1; it is not a refutation of every local calibrated inference.

### The decisive failure occurs inside ordinary empirical practice

The full animal-reason section makes causal and analogical attribution unusually confident. Hume moves from his consciously experienced purposive conduct to similar animal conduct, then to similar internal operations and causes. The first encountered animal can support this transition because relevant experience is already available; repeating that exact animal is unnecessary. He then requires theories of inference to fit animals as well as people, and explains their learning through custom rather than an argument for uniform nature ([T 1.3.16.1–9](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/16), SBN 176–179). Thus an indirect attribution can be definite without a reasoned foundation for the inferential mechanism. Neither definiteness nor absence of a foundation is exclusively a later phenomenon.

The sympathy section distinguishes stages often conflated. Observable effects and signs first cause an idea of another's affection. A causal judgment concerns the reality of that affection; resemblance and relations then enliven its idea into one's own passion. Another person's opinion can also affect one's judgment through argument rather than sympathy. At the close, undeserved praise and imagined acclaim can please without assent to their represented circumstances ([T 2.1.11.3–9, .19](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/2/1/11), SBN 317–320, 323–324). The section therefore supplies neither a direct transfer of another's feeling nor a guarantee that producing a feeling proves the original person's state. Its distinction between causal belief and sympathetic conversion matters to both M2 and M3.

The proposed generalization would say: independently experienced patterns license unobserved mental causes; therefore the same kind of evidence licenses ordinary objects as persisting independently of perception. Hume's porter and letter examples initially appear to support that generalization. Yet he immediately inspects their mechanism and says direct custom cannot increase regularity beyond the perceptions from which it arises. Believing bodies continued to exist supplies a greater regularity than the observed sequence itself. The imagination's momentum, supplemented by constancy, accounts for the extension ([T 1.4.2.19–24](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/2), SBN 195–199). This is not merely weak evidence for another instance of a directly sampled kind; it is a different construction of continuity across perceptual gaps.

The section's changes of standpoint are explicit. In .31 Hume adopts vulgar language identifying objects with perceptions. In .46 he announces the philosophical system of double existence, which inherits difficulties rather than receiving an independent causal proof. In .47 the required conjunction between perceptions and independent objects cannot be observed. After the destructive review, .57 proceeds under the supposition of both an internal and an external world ([T 1.4.2.31, .46–47, .53–57](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/2), SBN 201–202, 211–218). Quoting the letter's evidential force without .21–22, or reading later references to objects without .31 and .46, reverses the full argument.

A small countermodel isolates the missing premise. Suppose observations at times 0 and 2 give the same visible table and the same learned conjunctions. Model A includes the table throughout the unobserved interval; model B contains only the matching states at the two observation times. The specified observational data and conjunctions do not discriminate the models. A premise about persistence or continuity would do so, but it has not been supplied by those data alone. This is a countermodel to an entailment, not proof that Hume accepts model B or that real bodies disappear. Its historical relevance depends on Hume's own .21–22 distinction, not its formal construction.

The consequence is substantial for the proposed reconstruction. A learned human species, a credible witness, and an ordinary animal are identified within an established practice of object tracking. Their local use can be evidentially better than an arbitrary attribution without separately proving that practice's object ontology. Causal kind calibration therefore cannot bear the entire weight of an argument from other minds to independent bodies and minds. This finding withdraws the earlier ambition to resolve the science/suspension dispute through content restrictions alone. It does not imply that Hume disbelieves bodies, suspends ordinary judgment, or treats good science and superstition equally.

### The surviving restriction is stronger than a topic label

In the edited Enquiry, testimony depends on observed relations between reporting, memory, truthfulness, interest, manner, and the reported kind of event. Hume does not reduce credibility to number of witnesses. His imagined eight-day worldwide darkness may be accepted on convergent historical testimony, followed by inquiry into causes. Conversely, the supposed restoration of Elizabeth remains less credible than fraud or error despite extensive agreement ([EHU X.3–13, .35–38](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/10), SBN 110–116, 127–130). The causal comparison includes both reporting processes and event types.

The severe policy against religious miracle testimony in X.38 must nevertheless be retained. Adding a religious purpose makes testimony especially suspect because established tendencies toward enthusiasm and deceit alter the comparison. Nor may an alleged divine author's omnipotence raise the event's probability: attributes and actions themselves require evidence from usual productions. M1 captures a practical exclusion here, but misdescribes it as the initial premise of a categorical causal ban. M2 survives only if it incorporates this empirically defended exclusion, including its strong language, rather than replacing it with an unrestricted willingness to reconsider any witness count. How decisively the reported tendencies support a universal policy remains an additional evaluative question; no complete independent assessment of that generalization is claimed here.

The restriction on return inference in XI is stated for unconscious matter as well as intelligence. If a cause is estimated only from one effect, its estimated qualities cannot yield an independently warranted new effect. The friend impersonating Epicurus answers the narrator's building and footprint objections by appealing to acquaintance with human motives, projects, anatomy, and their connections. Those independent experiences license finishing the building or another footprint. The deity is not independently encountered as a member of such a sampled kind ([EHU XI.12–18, .24–27](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/11), SBN 136–139, 142–147). Possibility of additional qualities is conceded; their inference is denied. This is a limit on warrant and projected content, not a metaphysical proof of absent providence.

Speaker boundaries also restrict the result. The opening narrator distances himself from some of his friend's principles. At .28 he distinguishes what religious conclusions ought rationally to influence from what they actually influence. At .30 he adds a deeper objection: a cause entirely singular and wholly outside an experienced kind may not be inferable in the first place (SBN 147–148). The complete section therefore cannot be reduced to permission for a designer followed only by a ban on adding benevolence or afterlife. The first inferential step remains contested.

Cleanthes's articulate voice in Dialogues III is a serious test of that objection. Although unparalleled in its cosmic scale, it displays intelligible speech and instruction: an effect-type already connected with intelligence. His vegetating-book case likewise distinguishes reproduction from intelligible organization. Pamphilus reports Philo's difficulty; Demea's intervention is not a successful rebuttal by Philo ([D III.2–11](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/3)). Mere uniqueness is consequently too crude a defeater. The needed question is whether familiar discriminating features identify the relevant causal type, and which additional features the evidence warrants. It remains a question whether the world's order provides features as diagnostic as articulate speech. The thought experiment does not establish that equivalence.

In IV Philo challenges explanatory gain from installing an ordered mental world behind an ordered material one. Cleanthes rightly observes that explaining a cause's cause cannot be mandatory for every explanation. Philo accepts the legitimacy of explaining particular effects by general causes despite their ultimate obscurity; his objection is to an equally unexplained particular cause's alleged gain ([D IV.5–14](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/4)). This blocks an argument from ignorance of ultimate intrinsic nature to suspension of every causal explanation. It does not vindicate external-object inference against the different problem in T 1.4.2.

XII resists a uniform negative reading. Philo makes affirmative claims about design and proportional analogy; he distinguishes weaker moral from stronger natural attributes. He later restricts the surviving thesis to a remote analogy with human intelligence, with no licensed further qualities, effects, or practical consequences. His remarks about a philosopher looking beyond appearances and his descriptions of genuine sentiments are adverse to treating all affirmative language as mere concession. Cleanthes affirms religion's motivational force; Pamphilus finally ranks Cleanthes nearer truth than Philo ([D XII.2–9, .24–25, .33–34](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/12)). These voices cannot simply be collapsed. The thin conclusion defeats M1 as a comprehensive account, while its conditional presentation and the dialogue's tensions prevent its automatic promotion to Hume's settled unqualified commitment. The expression of philosophical assent also does not, by itself, decide the technical distinction between active and passive assent.

### The competing account of assent remains constrained

EHU XII permits correction of sensory appearances through circumstances such as distance, medium, and organs, then argues that a representational theory of independent external objects lacks the required experienced conjunction. Recourse to divine veracity is circular if proof of the deity already depends on the disputed external world. Its later discussion preserves natural belief and local inquiry despite unresolved inductive foundations; common life becomes methodized and corrected ([EHU XII.6–16, .22–25](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/12), SBN 151–155, 159–162). Local correction thus does not answer ultimate scepticism. The same section nevertheless allows theology a rational basis insofar as experience supports it (.32, SBN 165), another obstacle to M1.

M3 accommodates this combination better than foundational expansion of M2. But it earns no license to reinterpret every positive statement as a consciously withheld endorsement. Its explanatory obligation is to account for the correction and ranking Hume repeatedly makes, rather than treating every compelling transition alike. Neither religious enthusiasm nor undeserved praise becomes reliable merely by producing strong feeling. The complete [Natural History introduction](https://davidhume.org/texts/n/0) directly affirms a rational intelligent-author inference and refuses rational suspension, then distinguishes that question from the origin of variable popular beliefs. This is stronger affirmative evidence than a conditional conclusion from a dialogue speaker. It cannot be quietly reduced to the thin D XII concession or discarded as irony. The root reader additionally reports strong affirmative language in Moral Enquiry Appendix I; that unit has not been independently reread for this note and is recorded as reported adverse evidence, not added reading coverage. Such evidence reinforces the need to argue for passive assent rather than stipulating it. This assessment leaves qualified M3 live; it does not establish a globally passive Hume.

The chronological conclusion is correspondingly narrow. T 1.3.16 already combines confident attribution with custom and unexplained ultimate operation in 1739. T 1.4.2 already distinguishes inquiry under a supposition from proof of independent objects. The edited 1777 Enquiry repeats closely related object and induction difficulties. This establishes continuity of these particular problems, not a demonstrated developmental change or the absence of one. The 1748 witness has not been collated. The Dialogues appeared posthumously in 1779; their present edited text and speaker sequence cannot identify the date of each argumentative revision without manuscript collation.

The complete [Abstract](https://davidhume.org/texts/a/) supplies a 1740 control and an objection to our separation. Paragraph .27 classes belief in continued existence with causal belief as the same kind of sentiment. Does that unify the operations after all? It unifies their phenomenological character without establishing an identical formation history: T 1.4.2.21–22 expressly identifies the additional operation. The Abstract's .10 presents causal inference as a general explanatory operation, but its preface .iv identifies only one argument as completely traced and the remainder as hints. Its compression cannot silently retract the Treatise's qualification. Paragraphs .13–16 and .25 retain the unproved uniformity assumption and custom's role; .33 extends proportional causal inference to witnesses, history, and moral evidence. Paragraph .28 still asserts a composed train of perceptions without perfect simplicity or identity. These retain the causal account and the earlier union claim in the same work; they do not solve the Appendix's subsequent difficulty. The [editorial note](https://davidhume.org/texts/a/notes) identifies March 1740 publication and a 1740 copytext with some corrections from Hume's hand in a British Library copy. Those displayed corrections must not all be dated March 1740, and no composition-order inference between Abstract and Appendix is made here. The Natural History unit was read in its present edited state; its first 1757 witness was not collated, so its affirmation cannot by itself establish a dated conversion before the Dialogues.

### Original rivals and limits of contribution

Kim's original argument treats causal belief in another's mind separately from sympathy's ensuing passion. He defends analogy and general rules, questions a privileged first-person epistemic perspective using separable perceptions, and distinguishes the vivacity that produces other-mind belief from ordinary conjunction-driven belief. His recognition of that last difference prevents attributing to him the simplistic claim that every ordinary extension has one identical mechanism. The discussion of general rules and object belief is not, by itself, an ultimate foundation for independent objects. The present result therefore restricts our proposed bridge rather than refuting Kim's actual thesis ([Kim, original article](https://doi.org/10.1080/09608788.2018.1524365), original article pp. 1–21, especially 10–17; journal pp. 535–555). Complete original argument, notes, and bibliography were read through the displayed author text; page images were not collated.

Inoue presents sympathy as person perception, distinguishing it from contagion and simple inference. He addresses the objection that an idea of another's affection may suffice without its real occurrence, then develops a double association of ideas and impressions and a relational account of bodily ownership. His account has more structure than a bare analogy claim. Nevertheless, production of one's passion cannot by itself certify the original occurrence: ideas without assent can also affect us. The additional belief-producing relations remain necessary. Inoue himself raises the relevant objection, so it is not presented as a newly discovered refutation ([Inoue, original article](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jafpos1956/11/2/11_2_75/_pdf/-char/en), pp. 75–86, especially 79–86). All twelve pages, notes, and references were read from the preserved original.

No novelty claim is made for analogy, independently known human kinds, sceptical foundations, or the object-completion passages individually. The specific contribution to this project is a tested rejection of its own proposed unifying bridge, with a reason to carry separate models of local attribution, object completion, and assent. The broad model failed on an explicit adjacent qualification in the earlier text, not through model agreement or an inferred absence of evidence. A larger publishable claim would need comparison with full scholarship on external objects and assent, independent collation of early witnesses, and a sustained account of why Hume endorses some corrections. That work is not falsely represented as completed here.
