# Legitimate causal extension: a positive primary reconstruction

Date: 12 October 2026. Status: supported, limited reconstruction; not a discovery claim or a refutation of a named scholar. Separate investigation by the primary-reading agent. Companion: [reading ledger](positive-analogy-primary-v3-sources.json).

Hume permits inquiry into empirically supported relations while refusing unsupported claims about their bearers, additional attributes, or further effects. This distinction explains substantial differences between science, testimony and speculative theology without requiring every scientific assertion to be withdrawn in philosophical reflection. The proposed boundary between relational structure and intrinsic nature needs revision, however: some prohibited theological conclusions concern future actions and experienced happiness, not intrinsic nature. The stronger rule is to extend an inference only as far as independently supported similarities and the evidence for the particular conclusion permit. This is a reconstruction of several explicit arguments, not Hume's formulation of a single universal test.

The most decisive local evidence is *Treatise* 1.4.5.30 and .33, reinforced by *Enquiry* 11.24–27 and *Dialogues* 4.14. It establishes a positive separation of questions, not merely a psychological interruption of doubt. It does not settle the foundations of induction, the ontology of external objects, or Hume's personal religious belief.

## Reading and attribution limits

Read complete bounded sequences: T 1.4.5.1–35 and its notes; EHU sections 9–12, including their endnotes; Dialogues introduction and Parts 1–5 and 12, including notes. Dialogues Parts 6–11 were not read in this stage. The conclusion does not claim complete inspection of that work. All passages were inspected as Hume Texts Online's public HTML, with the displayed SBN/KS page labels. These page labels are verified in that transcription; the decisive passages have not been independently collated against page images in this stage. The result therefore remains an argument note rather than a publication-ready textual demonstration.

The Treatise witness starts from the original edition, incorporating errata and Appendix changes in the site's edited version; it is not a modern edition silently substituted for that witness. The first Enquiry was initially published in 1748; the website follows its 1777 text. The Dialogues text follows the first, posthumous 1779 edition. Its editor reports substantial composition in 1751–52 and revisions through 1776. Publication order alone consequently does not establish a late conversion or abandonment. No argument below uses the Dialogues to determine retrospectively what the Treatise meant. These provenance facts come from the site's [Treatise editorial notes](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/notes), [Enquiry editorial notes](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/notes) and [Dialogues editorial notes](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/notes); the latter's interpretation of Hume's religious conclusion is not adopted as evidence here.

EHU 11 reports a friend impersonating Epicurus, with the narrator explicitly withholding blanket approval (11.1). EHU 11.24 is the narrator's counterargument, 11.25–27 the friend's answer, and 11.28–30 the narrator's final interventions. The Dialogues records Cleanthes, Philo and Demea through Pamphilus, Cleanthes's pupil and effectively adopted son (D 1.1). Pamphilus's final preference for Cleanthes (D 12.34) must be retained, but is not a direct authorial announcement. Nor is any single utterance of Philo automatically Hume's final position.

## The local positive case

The decisive wording is Hume's conclusion that “motion may be, and actually is, the cause of thought and perception” (T 1.4.5.30, SBN 248), and Philo's grant: “Naturalists indeed very justly explain particular effects by more general causes” (D 4.14, KS 164). The surrounding arguments qualify both claims, as reconstructed below. These quotations reproduce the website's transcription and require page-image collation before publication.

**1. A cause can be identified without identifying a substance.** T 1.4.5.1–6 rejects the question of a material or immaterial substance supporting perceptions because the requisite idea and meaning have not been supplied. The subsequent discussions distinguish substance, local conjunction, and causation. At .29 Hume turns to the causes of perceptions as a more intelligible question. At .30 he explicitly requires separation of the substance of the mind from the causes of its thought; bodily dispositions change thoughts and sentiments, furnishing the relevant experienced union. He concludes that motion actually causes thought. At .33 the conclusion is expressly confined to the causal relation of which we have a notion. This is a claim within the account of causation, not proof that Hume identifies an inaccessible intrinsic productive mechanism. [T 1.4.5, SBN 232–251, especially 247–250](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/5).

This section also supplies a diagnostic of illicit enrichment. In .12–14, observed relations of causation and temporal contiguity between taste and visible/tangible qualities prompt an additional attribution of spatial conjunction. Hume rejects that added relation while preserving the others. The interpretive significance is precise: one relation's availability does not license an attractive further relation. The argument is not simply that all relations are knowable and all intrinsic properties unknowable.

**2. Extending beyond observation requires evidence of relevant resemblance, rather than direct observation of every target.** EHU 9.1 moves from anatomy in one animal to other animals, and then from human psychology to animal psychology. Behaviour under training, rewards, threats and familiar sounds provides evidence for learning and expectation (.2–5). The animal's own inference goes beyond present sensation; our explanation of its mind also extends from known phenomena by analogy. Neither requires direct access to the animal's private experience. This supports an ordinary, defeasible inference to mental operations; it is not a standalone solution to the philosophical problem of other minds. EHU 9's note H adds that experience can license an inference from a single carefully isolated experiment, using a previously acquired general habit; testimony enlarges the sphere of experience. Counting directly repeated target cases is consequently an inadequate rule. [EHU 9.1–6 and note H, SBN 104–108](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/9).

**3. Independent knowledge of a kind licenses more than one isolated effect licenses.** EHU 11.24 presents the unfinished building and a single footprint: apparently we infer a cause and then infer additional effects. The friend's response distinguishes the inference from a familiar human producer, known through many other observations, from a cause known solely through one effect. Human anatomy licenses the second foot; human purposes license further work on the building. The original footprint alone would support only a cause fitted to that impression. A divine producer has not independently been admitted to a familiar experienced species, so further intentions and productions lack this support (11.25–27). This is the clearest explanation of the difference between a warranted extension and an illicit addition. It is the friend's argument, but the narrator's final singular-universe objection at .30 reinforces rather than overturns the requirement for experienced resemblance between causal types. [EHU 11.24–30 and note M, SBN 142–148](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/11).

**4. Legitimate explanation need not remove ultimate ignorance.** Cleanthes argues that ordinary causal explanations are not invalidated by failure to explain the cause of their cause (D 4.13). Philo's reply expressly grants that naturalists justly explain particular effects through general causes which ultimately remain inexplicable (D 4.14). His objection concerns replacing one particular unexplained system with another, not the scientific practice he has just granted. Philo also answers Cleanthes's attempt to assimilate Copernican science to cosmic design by identifying observed planetary similarities and Galileo's work making celestial bodies proper objects of experience (D 2.25–28). Science enlarges an empirically supported class; theology's disputed move goes from local contrivance to the generation of the entire universe. [D 4.13–14, KS 163–164](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/4); [D 2.25–28, KS 150–151](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/2).

The inferential premises connecting these arguments are: **P1**, the distinction of causal question from substance question is deliberate and operative, rather than immediately cancelled by unspecified general scepticism; **P2**, the relevant resemblance must have evidential support, since formal describability alone gives no experience of conjunction; **P3**, one supported attribute does not license another merely because both occur together in familiar human cases; **P4**, claims about legitimate reasoning must be distinguished from reports about what people actually believe. P1 is especially well supported by the explicit separation in T 1.4.5.30 and grant in D 4.14. P2 and P3 are supported by EHU 11's contrast of independent species knowledge with a singular inferred cause. P4 is explicit in EHU 11.28. Combining these into one cross-work account is the interpretive step; no doctrinal identity across all revisions is presumed.

## Four applications, with their limits

| Application | What provides the empirical anchor | What the anchor does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Testimony and history | Experience of human veracity, motives, circumstances and the relation of reports to events; testimony itself then enlarges experience. | Every consistent report is true, or religious claims escape contrary evidence. |
| Animal learning and familiar human intentions | Behavioural similarities, training, anatomy, and independently experienced motives or operations. | An intrinsic mental substance, complete access to another mind, or transfer of every human trait to every inferred intelligence. |
| Scientific causes | Experienced causal union and experimentally supported similarity, allowing general causes to explain particular effects. | Ultimate productive mechanisms, an inference to any convenient unseen cause, or removal of foundational sceptical difficulties. |
| Cosmic design | At most a disputed, graded analogy from order to intelligence; Part 12 leaves room for very limited assent. | Infinity, perfection, unity, human passions, providential rewards or arbitrary future interventions. |

Testimony is not an exception to causal inference: EHU 10.5–7 derives its force from experience of reports, veracity, motives and circumstances. The exceptional hypothetical darkness of 1600 (10.36) should be accepted on uniform worldwide evidence, while philosophers search for its cause. This supports unseen historical events, including extraordinary ones. But Hume withholds comparable assent to the queen's resurrection (10.37), and denies that inferred divine attributes can independently inflate the credibility of miraculous reports (10.38). The note on unfamiliar frost (10.10, note I) warns against treating lack of experience under new circumstances as contrary uniform experience. The exact evidential balance matters; novelty alone does not prohibit belief. [EHU 10.5–10, note I, and 10.35–38, SBN 111–114, 127–129](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/10).

The external-object argument is a harder boundary. EHU 12.10–14 asks how perceptions could establish resembling external causes when no conjunction between perceptions and external objects is ever experienced. A proposed relational structure is not enough: the alleged relata have never been jointly available to experience in the required way. This supports a restriction on that philosophical inference, not a proof that external objects do not exist. EHU 12.16's unknown substratum objection further challenges intelligible content after sensible qualities are removed. The causal claims made in science cannot simply be assumed to solve this foundational problem. [EHU 12.10–16, SBN 152–155](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/12).

## Counterevidence that constrains the reconstruction

**Cleanthes's novel intelligent voice remains a serious test.** D 3.2–3 imagines an articulate voice from the clouds, speaking to every nation in its own language. Its novelty and magnitude should not prevent inferring intelligence; Cleanthes uses it to challenge Philo's objection to dissimilar cases. D 3.10 reports Philo embarrassed and hesitating before Demea interrupts. The text gives no Philo reply to this case. A reconstructed answer can distinguish novelty of size or location from the familiar causal signature of articulated language: an intelligent cause could be inferred without infinity or a complete divine psychology. But that answer is ours, not a recorded victory for Philo. It also concedes Cleanthes's consequential point that direct repeated experience of the whole target situation is unnecessary. [D 3.2–10, KS 152–155](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/3).

**A singular universe is more than an unknown essence.** Philo's D 2.17–24 objects to transfers across circumstances, parts and wholes, the existing world and its formation. EHU 11.30's narrator asks whether a wholly singular cause and effect permit any conjecture. This stronger challenge could block even thin cosmic design, whereas the voice retains similarities to familiar speech. Conversely, D 12.2–4 strongly links scientific anatomy and astronomy to an intelligent author, and .6 describes the Deity's existence as established by reason. These are consequential positive assertions, not merely failures to suppress a natural belief. D 12.7–8 and .33 qualify the analogy's content, distinguish natural from moral attributes, and restrict further specification. Part 12 thus cannot be treated as complete suspension; nor can its earlier strong affirmations simply be erased by concentrating on the restricted final proposition. Its relation to earlier stronger objections is unresolved by this note. D 5.5–12 does nevertheless explain why even granting design does not establish infinity, perfection, one designer, or the designer's condition. [D 2.17–24, KS 147–150](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/2); [D 5.5–13, KS 166–169](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/5); [D 12.2–8, .33–34, KS 214–219, 227–228](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/12).

**Argument limits do not establish actual suspension.** EHU 11.28 explicitly distinguishes an argument that religion ought not affect conduct from the empirical fact that it does. EHU 12.22–25 describes the fragility of excessive scepticism and the survival of moderated caution. D 12.6 denies the possibility of sustained suspension over evident design; D 12's note 11 recognizes unavoidable thinking and assent despite insoluble logical difficulties. D 1.11's prediction of suspense in remote theological inquiry therefore cannot simply be generalized to all science or substituted for the contrary psychological claims elsewhere. Nor may Part 12's assent be silently replaced by the critic's view that its content is meagre. [EHU 11.28, SBN 146–147](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/11); [EHU 12.22–25, SBN 159–162](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/12); [D 1.8–13, KS 133–137](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/1); [D 12.6 and note 11, KS 216–217, 219](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/12).

## Precise result

The positive result is that Hume can affirm an empirically warranted causal relation while rejecting the meaningfulness or warrant of a further substance claim. He can also extend an inference to an unseen case when relevant resemblance and independent knowledge of the causal kind support the particular addition. Unknown ultimate explanation alone does not bar science. These claims have explicit local textual support.

The stronger proposal that Hume bars only inference to ultimate intrinsic nature fails: EHU 11 also bars unsupported additions about future effects, and cosmic design faces a disputed analogy problem before its intrinsic nature is considered. A better reconstruction combines intelligible causal content, experienced resemblance, proportional strength and independent evidence for added attributes. It makes a substantial part of the science/speculation contrast intelligible without global changes of standpoint; it does not eliminate the sceptical problem of justification or establish Hume's actual universal suspension. The analogical component is established terrain, not a novel discovery. No priority or scholarship-level novelty assessment is claimed in this primary-reading stage.
