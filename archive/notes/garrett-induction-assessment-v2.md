# Garrett and Millican on induction: separate adversarial assessment, v2

Date: 12 October 2026. Status: completed separate assessment, with bounded literature coverage. This is an assessment of a substantial existing dispute, not a discovery claim. Scope is the explanation of induction and the boundary of reason; no claim about Hakkarainen is made here.

## Precise question and why it matters

Does Hume exclude reasoning as the source of the indispensable transition to the unobserved, even when the reasoning is mistaken, or exclude an independently warranted rational foundation for that transition? The difference affects whether custom supplies a psychological explanation, an epistemic entitlement, or both. It also changes the burden of proof: a causal impossibility claim must handle persuasive fallacies; a claim about warrant may disregard them precisely because they fail to warrant their conclusions.

Three propositions must be kept separate:

1. Ordinary prediction does not require a prior argument for a principle of uniformity.
2. Every inductive episode depends on custom somewhere in the transition or its belief-producing basis.
3. No argument can immediately cause a particular prediction, including when an adult explicitly applies a previously accepted uniformity premise.

The first has excellent primary support. The second has substantial support, including reflective cases. The third is considerably stronger; it cannot be obtained by relabelling an invalid step or citing the historical origin of a premise.

## The actual exchange

The [complete 2011 dialogue](https://era.ed.ac.uk/bitstreams/ce5776f7-d5c1-4972-bc50-4952a296ac36/download) was inspected in full, including questions and bibliography. Relevant disagreement: printed pp17, 20–24. Garrett accepts bad probable reasoning and reason's fallibility; he does not exclude every false belief from reason. He assigns an invalid part of an attempted demonstration to narrow imagination, while allowing genuine inference elsewhere in the attempt. His exact p24 formulation is: “The bad part is not some demonstration.” Millican objects that an unsuccessful argument can still causally produce belief. Both accept epistemic consequences, default authority for natural faculties, and Hume's own employment of reason in the argument. Garrett also permits complex inference: the disputed exclusion concerns its indispensable inductive transition, not every constituent operation. His account of understanding permits intuition (p19). These concessions rule out the easy criticisms that he ignores errors, prohibits reflection, or thinks induction immune from epistemic appraisal.

## Tests against complete primary sequences

The following arguments are my reconstruction from Hume, not additional paraphrase of the dialogue.

### 1. The negative argument has an explanatory target

[T1.3.6](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/6), read completely, asks whether experience produces the relevant idea through understanding or imagination (.4; SBN88–89). At .7 (SBN90) the causal circularity formulation is especially favorable to Garrett: the same principle cannot be both cause and effect of another. At .12–16 Hume passes from the failure of reason to association, then explicitly contrasts reasoning and the association of perceptions. These are substantial causal commitments. Treating the passage as an exclusively normative demand loses that argumentative sequence.

But the sequence does not make the causal and epistemic claims mutually exclusive. Its assertions about proof, certainty and the presumption on which probability is founded can constrain a legitimate source of belief as well as describe a mechanism. The causal formulation alone cannot settle which constraint governs the exclusion of alternative mechanisms.

[E4](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/4), also read completely, sharpens both sides. At .16 (SBN33–34) Hume permits the inference to be just, while asking for the connecting process. This supports Garrett's distinction between inference and an argument that explains it; it is not an unconditional endorsement settling the whole epistemology. At .23 (SBN39) children and animals supply a powerful explanatory test. An elaborate demonstration cannot be a necessary mechanism common to every ordinary prediction, since subjects lacking that capacity nevertheless predict. Their capacities do not establish that an adult's special prediction cannot be affected by an argument. The universal claim about a shared necessary mechanism and a universal claim excluding every alternative proximate cause are different.

### 2. The one-experiment case is the strongest test of the universal claim

[T1.3.8.13–14](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/8) (SBN103–105), inspected within the complete section, first describes prediction occurring before reflection. Hume then expressly admits that reflection sometimes produces belief without the specific habit—or, as he corrects himself, produces custom indirectly. A carefully controlled single experiment can support a new prediction because a more general principle of uniform effects has already become habitual through many observations. Hume ends by requiring a transfer of experience, express or tacit, direct or indirect.

This is positive evidence for Garrett. Custom is not simply absent when a reasoner reflects. Hume deliberately reinstates it within the explanation of a reflective case, and the habitual general principle contributes present firmness to the opinion. This is more than a biographical fact about how the premise was once acquired.

Nevertheless, it does not exclude inference as another proximate cause. An adult may explicitly combine an established general principle with a present experimental result. An account that permits this reasoning to direct the prediction while custom supplies the general principle's conviction fits Hume's correction. Thus the paragraph favors proposition 2 without establishing proposition 3. To defend proposition 3, Garrett would need an independently evidenced distinction between an inferentially articulated prediction and the production of its assent. That distinction cannot be supplied solely by calling the earlier acquisition of the principle part of the present inference.

[E5](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/5), read completely with notes, reinforces this qualified defence. At .2 (SBN41–42), Hume says there is a step unsupported by argument in all experiential reasoning, rather than saying no reasoning contains an argument. At .5 and .20 (SBN43,53–54), custom supplies the explanatory transition. NoteB (.5n8.1–5; SBN43–45) allows elaborate reflection while tracing it ultimately to experience. These passages favor a dependency analysis of an episode's indispensable basis. They do not warrant denying the causal efficacy of its other operations.

### 3. Mistaken demonstration: Garrett has a real distinction, but a further burden

[T1.4.1](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/1), read completely, opens by distinguishing infallible demonstrative rules from our fallible application of them (.1; SBN180). Reason is a cause whose natural effect is truth, but interference and instability can prevent that effect. A genuine grasp of a demonstrative relation and a mistaken performance need not be the same operation. This gives Garrett textual grounds for decomposing an attempted proof rather than treating invalidity as a successful demonstration.

The difficulty is the move from **not a demonstration** to **not reason**. The paragraph describes errors within the employment of understanding, and its reflexive checks assess the faculty's reliability. It does not explicitly assign every invalid step to narrow imagination. Hume's causal vocabulary also permits reason's operation to be disrupted; this does not by itself show that the faulty cognitive episode is wholly outside reason. Both the decomposition and the attribution to fallible reason require interpretation.

[T1.3.3](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/3), read completely, gives concrete examples. Hume first denies a genuine demonstration of the causal maxim, then separately exposes plausible purported demonstrations (.4–8; SBN80–82). They confuse absence of a cause with self-causation or causation by nothing. The section shows that the absence of a valid demonstration does not make the examination of particular persuasive fallacies redundant. At .9, Hume sends the opinion's explanation to observation and experience. That supports a role for experience, but does not supply an exhaustive psychology of how the erroneous demonstrations persuade individual philosophers.

The strongest pressure is [E5.22](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/5) (SBN55), in the positive explanatory phase itself:

> it is not probable, that it could be trusted to the fallacious deductions of our reason, which is slow in its operations

Hume calls reason fallible while contrasting it with the quick, early and reliable operation needed for survival. This defeats a blanket interpretation under which only truth-producing operations count as reason. **It does not alone defeat Garrett's actual position:** he already allows mistaken probable inference, and “deductions” here need not mean exclusively purported demonstration. Nor does it conclusively establish whether the paragraph praises custom's truth-conduciveness or its stable causal operation. It does place the burden on anyone assigning an invalid demonstrative step to imagination: identify why that step differs in faculty from the fallacious reasoning Hume expressly acknowledges.

Selective inspection of [T1.2.4.7–23](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/2/4) (especially .11/.17; SBN43–45) confirms the distinction between genuine and purported demonstration. It gives no automatic licence to equate every failed performance with absence of reason.

### 4. The imagination footnote cannot settle the entire issue alone

[T1.3.9](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/9), read completely, includes education-induced conviction (.16–19; SBN115–117) and the footnote distinguishing broad imagination from imagination excluding demonstrative and probable reasonings (.19n22; SBN117–118). Education is an effective belief-producing mechanism without becoming an inference merely because it causes assent. That supports Garrett against treating every cognitive transition as reasoning.

However, [T1.3.13](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/13) and [T1.4.4](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/4), both read completely, introduce distinctions between more constant and less regular imaginative principles, with explicitly favorable evaluations of disciplined inference. These distinctions are evaluative and causal at once. They do not establish a simple equivalence between the whole realm of inference and reliable judgment.

[T1.4.7](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/7), read completely, must constrain both readings. The dilemma of .6–8 (SBN267–269) prevents a comfortable equation of established imaginative principles with permanently guaranteed epistemic safety. Its eventual return to philosophy and selective assent (.11–15; SBN270–274) also prevents a wholesale rejection of reason. A faculty taxonomy and a hierarchy of reliable processes need not be identical; neither may be imposed without accounting for this sequence.

## Later objections and the existing literature

The [original Garrett2014 reply, published2016](https://humesociety.org/?hume_article_pdf=16383), was subsequently read completely: cover plus printed pp227–242, including notes. At pp234–235 Garrett distinguishes the footnote's inferential boundary from a distinction **within** unreasoning imagination between stable custom and irregular principles. At pp235–236 he accepts a classificatory difficulty about immediate intuition; intuitive inferences can be short demonstrations, while non-inferential intuition belongs to understanding. At pp236–238 he distinguishes supplying cognitive input from being an operation of reason. His notes6–7 (pp240–241) explicitly address unreliable imaginative influences and the dangerous dilemma. This reply does not newly answer the particular bad-demonstration objection or analyze E5.22. It does answer the criticism that he neglects the later imagination passages. The original printed reference at p236 to T2.2.3 in the discussion of voluntary action is an apparent miscitation; T2.3.3 is the relevant sequence, and no inference here relies on the misprinted identifier.

This changes the best adversarial test. From Hume's own text, the customary transition belongs to imagination rather than reason at T1.3.6.12–15, and to the permanent class at T1.4.4.1. If narrow imagination simply means the irregular class, these two assignments conflict. Garrett's two distinctions avoid that conflict: a non-argumentative process can be stable, and a reasoning episode can depend on it. Hume's explicit inferential boundary in the footnote supports this separation. The separate normative boundary then evaluates processes without converting every well-functioning process into reason.

This is positive support for a consequential part of Garrett's actual interpretation, not just a common commitment to custom. It explains how Hume can exclude a mechanism from reason without thereby condemning its epistemic contribution. The strongest counterevidence is T1.4.7.7's identification of understanding with more established imaginative properties. Garrett's reading can treat understanding's dependence on those properties as the intended sense, but that is an interpretive expansion of Hume's identifying phrase. The immediate-intuition concession also shows that the footnote is not a completely exhaustive classification. Therefore the separation of boundaries is better supported than a categorical exclusion of every mistaken argumentative performance from reason.

[Millican2012](https://davidhume.org/scholarship/papers/millican/2012_Induction.pdf), selectively inspected pp74–83 and the beginning of84, develops two consequential objections: fallacious apparent demonstrations remain possible causes; and applying a previously established uniformity premise differs psychologically from establishing it. He also outlines a broader cognitive faculty with stricter and looser applications of its name. The latter accommodates fallibility while explaining epistemically restricted uses. Its historical faculty comparisons were inspected in the chapter, not independently verified in their source editions.

[Millican2014](https://davidhume.org/scholarship/papers/millican/2014_Garrett%27s_Hume.pdf), selectively inspected pp209–216 and relevant notes on222–223, presses the relation between the imagination footnote and the later distinction between stable principles and caprice. His proposed reading makes reason's boundary partly normative. His interpretation requires explaining the literal inferential boundary in the footnote; brevity and editorial circumstances do not themselves prove inaccuracy. Conversely, the footnote cannot make the subsequent evaluative passages disappear.

The bad-argument objection is established scholarship, not an HPA novelty. [Loeb2006's author manuscript](https://fitelson.org/confirmation/loeb.pdf), selectively inspected manuscript pp9–13, explicitly endorses Millican's objection and cites Garrett1998p187. Loeb proposes that a causal explanation must preserve the justification of the explained inferences. The 1998 originals were not independently read here; their reported positions must not substitute for the 2011 clarification.

[Qu's early online version](https://pgrim.org/philosophersannual/2014nominations/quhume.pdf), selectively inspected pp1–3,9–10 and endnotes25–27, uses the positive argument to support a normative reading. Note27 reports Garrett's alternative reading of E5.22 as praise of custom's causal reliability. This is evidence that the objection and its main defence were already considered, not permission to attribute a new published commitment to Garrett from correspondence.

[Cottrell2024](https://www.research.ed.ac.uk/files/471204480/jmp-1901-cottrell_1_.pdf), selectively inspected pp1–3,5–9 and bibliographic metadata, argues that foundation has causal and evaluative force together. His use of E5.7 is especially relevant: a belief lacking a sensory or memorial foundation need not lack a psychological cause. This is a substantial live alternative to a strict separation of causal explanation and epistemic warrant. The full natural-teleology argument was not evaluated here.

## Explicit premises, balance, and falsification conditions

The strongest defence of Garrett rests on three interpretive premises: (a) Hume's explanatory question targets the operation supplying the indispensable transition or its conviction, rather than every causal contributor; (b) the distinction between an inference and an argument explaining it is genuine; and (c) a mechanism can possess defeasible authority without acquiring it from an independent proof. Premises(a) and(b) receive direct support from the reflective case and E5.2; premise(c) remains a broader epistemological commitment, not a consequence of the induction argument alone.

My balance favors Garrett's **dependency claim and distinction between inferential and evaluative boundaries** over the objection that the mere possibility of bad arguments destroys his explanation. The primary sequence supplies positive evidence for custom even in reflective cases. It favors Millican on the need to account for normative constraints and on the insufficiency of excluding invalid demonstration by terminology alone. The apparent demonstrative fallacy remains a pressure point, not an established refutation of the strengthened causal interpretation. This balance supports a substantive side of the dispute without claiming either interpretation has been completely vindicated.

Two tests could change it. First, a passage in which Hume explicitly allows a single prediction to depend solely on an antecedently believed uniformity premise, with no custom supplying its present conviction, would undermine the episode-level dependency defence. Second, a passage explicitly attributing the invalid inferential step of an apparent demonstration to narrow imagination, as opposed to fallible reasoning, would strengthen Garrett's taxonomy. Neither has been established in the inspected sequences. These are textual tests, not suggested experiments or questions to authors.

**Limits:** the latest reply inspected here is the2014 issue article that appeared2016, not a proof that no later reply exists. Garrett's2015 book, the original1998 exchange and his other treatments of the Title Principle were not read completely in this assessment. The2014/2016 reply explicitly refers to those works. The general objection and main replies are already in the literature; neither novelty nor an unreplied refutation is claimed.
