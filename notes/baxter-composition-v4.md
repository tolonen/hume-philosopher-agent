**Verdict:** retain a genuine ontological fork between organized plurality and a real complex being. ## The strongest accessible original argument

The complete [Baxter substance chapter](https://philarchive.org/archive/BAXHOS-4), a 27-page penultimate manuscript dated 6 January 2014 for a 2015 volume, denies complex individual substances in the strict sense. Its strongest reply is not that relations are unreal. Related parts can exist collectively without a further unitary entity (pp. 6–7). The dependence interpretation is expressly considered (pp. 7–10). Real compound ideas and compounded objects are anticipated, with Hakkarainen acknowledged in note 16. Sections V–VI combine mental separability, conceivability, and independence; a modal distinction is considered rather than overlooked. The explicit answer to Hakkarainen 2012, pp. 56–57, is on manuscript pp. 25–26: dependence of a whole on its parts describes their collective existence, not asymmetric dependence between two strictly existing relata. The consequential claim denies asymmetric inseparability between strictly existing things (paraphrase; manuscript pp. 25–26). The closing explanation invokes unity/being, real separability, and Leibniz's Law (p. 27). This already addresses familiar whole–part counterexamples; presenting those examples as a fresh refutation would miss the argument.

## A primary-text test that does not assume its answer

The most discriminating question is whether the phrase *existence in itself* in T 1.2.2.3 restricts all genuine existence to indivisible units, or restricts independent existence while allowing a dependent composite. The surrounding argument is about the impossibility of infinite divisibility; it explicitly groups determinate extension, twenty men, the globe, and the universe under fictitious unity. A realist cannot simply exempt determinate extension because it looks more integrated than twenty men. Conversely, the qualification about existence in itself and the claim that number exists because its units exist leave a question about derivative existence. [Complete T 1.2.2, SBN 29–33](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/2/2).

T 1.2.3.14 calls the compound idea of extension real and insists that nonentities cannot compose it. T 1.2.4.6 says the union of contiguous points results in a compounded, divisible object while each part retains its distinct existence. These passages establish real components and intelligible arrangement. Whether they establish an additional single being is precisely the disputed step. The phrase *results an object* is serious realist evidence; the simultaneous insistence on distinct component existence supports a collective interpretation. Neither ordinary singular grammar nor the word *real* alone resolves how many beings Hume admits. [Complete T 1.2.3, SBN 33–39](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/2/3); [bounded T 1.2.4.1–11, especially .6, SBN 41](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/2/4).

The other decisive test is the domain of T 1.4.5.5. Its conclusion extends the independence definition of substance to everything conceivable. If that includes each whole as an entity distinct from every part, whole–part dependence conflicts with the principle. If separability concerns completely distinct existences, the whole–part relation may fall outside that domain. That restriction requires textual support; so does the inference that every conceivable composite is only many things. The appeal to conceivability cannot settle the dispute before the relata have been individuated. A possible partial distinction therefore bears directly on the argument's applicability rather than merely adding unexplained terminology. [Complete T 1.4.5, SBN 232–251](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/5), read in the preceding stage and retained as a bounded source here.

There is later corroboration for a collective interpretation: Cleanthes says that grouping a succession into a whole is an arbitrary mental act, and that causes of twenty particles sufficiently explain the twenty collectively. Philo permits Cleanthes's criticisms to stand before adding another objection. This remains a character's argument against a demand for an additional cosmic cause, not an unqualified narrator's endorsement of a complete ontology. [Complete Dialogues IX, especially .9–10, KS 190–191](https://davidhume.org/texts/d/9).

## Temporal identity and the remaining difficulty

For the temporal interpretation, a complete accessible original is Baxter's six-page [2009 précis](https://philarchive.org/archive/BAXPOH-2), identified as a manuscript for *Philosophical Studies* 146, 407–411. It distinguishes discrete moments, a steadfast object coexisting with a succession, and duration understood as succession (manuscript pp. 1–3). Alternating ways of regarding an object disguise rather than remove a conflict between unity and number. Its proposed explanation of the personal-identity retraction appeals to consciousness: an invented internal source of unity cannot escape introspection in the way an unperceived body can (pp. 3–4). Its final discussion distinguishes practical and causal convincingness from epistemic justification (pp. 4–6). This is the author's own reconstruction, not a full reading of the book or its critics' responses. It prevents attributing to him the crude thesis that an unchanged object must be a succession merely because something else changes beside it.

The primary mass example supplies a particularly good control. T 1.4.6.8 allows perfect identity when every part remains uninterruptedly and invariably the same, notwithstanding motion of the parts or whole. The statement is a supposition and does not automatically establish an additional composite entity. Yet it blocks the inference that mere complexity or any change whatsoever precludes identity. T 1.4.6.11–14 explains ordinary identity under replacement by purpose, organic causal relations, and expected changes. T 1.4.6.19 describes a person as a causal system and allows changed character. These are substantial commitments to description and explanation, while .16 distinguishes them from strict identity. [Complete T 1.4.6, SBN 251–263](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/6).

This yields a limited integration with HPA-006. A physiological event can cause a thought without an account of the substance in which thought inheres (T 1.4.5.30, .33). A causal history can support an attribution to an organism or person without asserting unchanged constituents or an indivisible soul. It does not follow that causal organization constitutes one additional being, or that it solves the unity of consciousness. App.20–21 expressly records failure to explain what unites successive perceptions while retaining their distinct existence and the lack of perceived real connection. [Complete Appendix, SBN 623–636](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/app).
