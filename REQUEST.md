Establish and begin the **Hume Philosopher Agent (HPA)** project.

Our research question is how far an LLM can advance substantive history of philosophy through sustained reading, accurate reconstruction and rigorous criticism of existing scholarship. Our immediate objective is to find consequential errors in scholarly interpretations of Hume, demonstrate those errors from Hume’s works, and develop better readings of the disputed passages.

This is a research undertaking aimed at potential scholarly interventions. Do not deliver a general survey, a collection of speculative objections or fluent summaries presented as discoveries. Build the necessary research apparatus and begin substantive investigations.

**1. The objective and standard of success**

Investigate identifiable claims in published scholarship: what a particular author argues, about which passages, through which reasoning, and with what consequences.

Prioritise mistakes that materially affect an interpretation: a false textual premise, an equivocation, an invalid inference, a reversal of an argument’s function, an unsupported transition between different senses of a concept, or a reading contradicted by relevant primary evidence.

A finding must establish:

- The scholar actually makes the disputed claim.
- The objection addresses the strongest reasonable version of that claim.
- The relevant evidence comes from Hume’s works.
- The reasoning from evidence to objection is explicit.
- The objection survives serious attempts to answer it.
- An alternative reading explains the disputed passage and the evidence that defeats the original interpretation.
- The mistake matters beyond a minor correction.

Do not assume that scholars must be wrong. A careful investigation that vindicates an argument or establishes genuine underdetermination is a valid research result. Never manufacture a discovery to satisfy the objective.

We seek the strongest achievable demonstration, not numerical confidence scores or declarations of certainty. Distinguish what the text establishes, what follows logically, and what remains interpretive.

**2. Build a source-grounded research foundation**

Inspect the existing Hume research materials, repositories and available infrastructure first. Reuse verified resources and establish a distinct HPA project within the existing research environment. Keep this work separate from manuscript-discovery spiders.

Use GitHub for the research record, code, bibliography, argument dossiers and progress. Preserve supporting materials in the existing authorised private research storage. Do not publicly distribute restricted source files or contact scholars.

Build a searchable, versioned primary corpus, beginning with the complete *Treatise*, including its Appendix, and the *Abstract*. Include the two *Enquiries*, essays, *Dialogues*, correspondence and other works as the investigations require. Hume Texts Online is a starting resource, not a substitute for checking editorial provenance.

For every evidential passage preserve:

- Exact wording and sufficient surrounding context.
- Work, edition and stable section or paragraph reference.
- Page reference where applicable and verified.
- Textual variants or editorial interventions relevant to the argument.
- A retrievable source location.

Do not silently combine editions or modernise wording that bears on the interpretation. Verify decisive quotations against a reliable edition or page image where available.

Chronology matters. A later passage may clarify, revise or abandon an earlier position. It does not automatically determine what the earlier work meant. Equally, do not invoke intellectual development simply to remove a contradiction: establish the evidence for it.

**3. Read scholarship deeply enough to reconstruct its arguments**

Begin with disputes around the *Treatise*, especially Books 1 and 2. Include the New Hume debate, but do not organise the project around proving one established school correct.

Develop a broad dispute register covering, initially:

- Causation, necessary connection and causal powers.
- Induction, causal inference and the scope of reason.
- Ideas, impressions, representation and the copy principle.
- Belief, imagination and distinctions between kinds of assent.
- Scepticism, external objects and continued existence.
- Personal identity and the Appendix.
- Reason, passion, motivation and practical deliberation.
- Liberty, necessity and their relationship to Hume’s account of causation.

These are research areas, not predetermined diagnoses. Expand the register when the literature justifies it.

Read original articles and relevant book chapters, their cited primary passages, and substantive responses. Search for later qualifications, revised formulations and replies before criticising an earlier formulation. Read enough surrounding material to understand the author’s terminology and argumentative purpose.

Use bibliographies, reviews and surveys to discover arguments, but do not treat a summary or abstract as sufficient evidence of what a scholar argues. Mark inaccessible arguments as unassessed.

Maintain a reading record that distinguishes full argument inspection from partial access. Build durable notes with source references rather than relying on conversational memory.

**4. Make individual claims the unit of investigation**

For each candidate, record:

- Author, publication, date and exact page.
- A short exact quotation establishing the claim.
- Its context and qualifications.
- The passages of Hume it interprets.
- Its premises, intermediate steps and conclusion.
- Whether it concerns Hume’s meaning, beliefs, argumentative commitments or the philosophical success of his argument.
- What would have to be established to defeat it.

Distinguish “Hume asserts X”, “Hume’s argument requires X”, “Hume’s position permits X” and “X is the best reconstruction of Hume”. These require different objections.

Separate criticisms of Hume from criticisms of a scholar’s reading of Hume. Showing that a doctrine is philosophically unattractive does not show that Hume did not hold it. Showing that a scholar’s argument fails does not by itself show that their conclusion is false.

**5. Construct an explicit demonstration for every serious candidate**

Each dossier must contain a reconstructable chain:

**Target claim → primary evidence → interpretive premises → inference → precise conclusion.**

Label the interpretive premises instead of hiding them inside paraphrases. Identify which are shared with the target scholar and which are contested.

Pay particular attention to:

- Changes in the meaning of terms across an argument.
- Negation, quantifiers, modality and the scope of claims.
- Differences between knowledge, belief, supposition and conceivability.
- Differences between psychological explanation and epistemic justification.
- Differences between claims about ideas and claims about their objects.
- Hume’s own assertions versus objections, concessions, reported views and reductio arguments.
- Local argumentative claims versus general doctrinal commitments.
- Apparent contradictions arising from different works, periods or argumentative settings.

A failed argument is not a refuted conclusion. An absent statement is not a denial. Compatibility is not commitment. One counterexample defeats a universal claim only if it falls within the claim’s actual scope.

Where useful, formalise an inference and check it with logical tools. Such checking establishes consequences of the formalised premises; it does not establish that the formalisation accurately represents Hume or the scholar. Explain that correspondence separately.

Search methods and similarity scores may locate evidence. They must never constitute the warrant for a verdict.

**6. Try seriously to defeat every proposed objection**

Before promoting a finding, reconstruct the strongest reply available to the criticised scholar.

Search deliberately for primary passages that support their position. Examine their treatment of apparent counterevidence. Test alternative meanings, local context, qualifications, chronology and the possibility that your objection targets a claim they never made.

Use a separate adversarial assessment, where available, supplied with the source material and the target argument without being instructed to agree with the proposed objection. Ask it to defend the scholar and identify the weakest step in our reconstruction. Agreement between models is not independent historical evidence.

Maintain an objection-and-response record. If a credible defence survives, narrow the conclusion or classify the issue as unresolved. Do not label every surviving disagreement a grave error.

Distinguish explicitly:

- Demonstrable misquotation or misattribution.
- Demonstrable failure of a particular inference.
- Textual contradiction that survives contextual checking.
- An interpretation weakened by counterevidence.
- A competing interpretation that remains contestable.
- A candidate objection withdrawn after investigation.

Only the strongest cases should become proposed interventions.

**7. Produce a positive alternative reading**

For every surviving consequential objection, explain what Hume is doing in the disputed passage and how the argument works.

The alternative must account for the passage’s wording, its immediate argumentative sequence, relevant parallels and the strongest evidence supporting the rejected interpretation. State what changes in our understanding of Hume if the alternative succeeds.

Do not rely on repeatedly declaring Hume inconsistent, careless or ironic to save our reading. Such explanations require evidence. Nor should assumed consistency force genuinely conflicting passages into artificial agreement.

An objection may establish that an existing reading fails without uniquely establishing our replacement. State this distinction. Where alternatives remain viable, compare them and identify the evidence that would discriminate between them.

**8. Establish novelty and scholarly consequence**

Before describing an objection as a discovery, search the relevant literature for the same argument, textual observation and alternative reading. Follow citations in both directions where possible.

Distinguish:

- An already published objection reproduced successfully.
- A stronger or more complete demonstration of a known objection.
- New evidence bearing on an existing disagreement.
- A potentially original objection or reconstruction.

Finding a familiar criticism independently is useful for evaluating the agent, but is not an original scholarly intervention. Do not equate failure to locate a precedent with proof that none exists. Record the search coverage.

Explain the consequence precisely: which claim fails, which downstream arguments depend on it, and which parts of the scholar’s interpretation remain intact. Do not inflate a local correction into the collapse of an entire position.

**9. Evaluate the agent as well as the arguments**

Preserve model and configuration details, source access, important prompts, successive argument versions, rejected hypotheses and the reasons verdicts changed.

Separate source-grounded findings from unsupported recollection. Do not claim that reproducing an established objection demonstrates novel reasoning or that a model necessarily had no prior exposure to the debate.

Maintain known disputes as useful comparison cases, while identifying potentially original findings separately. Any evaluation set must remain separate from material used to develop the method, as far as practicable.

Label all automated assessments honestly. Prepare the strongest cases for expert scrutiny without fabricating human endorsement. A verified citation and a valid inference are necessary components of a finding, not automatic certification of an entire interpretation.

**10. Begin substantive work now**

Do not spend the entire first phase building infrastructure or promising comprehensive reading. Establish the minimum reliable source and argument records, map the initial disputes, and select a small first set of consequential claims whose full arguments and primary evidence are accessible.

Start with the New Hume debate and at least one distinct problem in the *Treatise* so that the method is tested beyond a single controversy. Select exact claims only after inspecting the publications.

Complete the first investigations through objection, strongest defence and alternative reading. Broaden coverage as the method becomes reliable. Do not impose a quota of refutations or repeatedly revisit a closed candidate without new evidence.

The first substantive delivery should contain:

- The source and scholarship coverage actually achieved.
- The initial dispute register with exact claims and references.
- Completed argument dossiers, including unsuccessful objections.
- The strongest surviving finding, if any, with its full evidential chain.
- Its best counterargument and our response.
- The alternative reading and its remaining limitations.
- The novelty check and the specific scholarly consequence.
- The next investigations justified by the results.

If no decisive finding survives, report that plainly and continue with other well-founded candidates. The ambition is substantial: establish how far an LLM can produce original, defensible scholarship on Hume. The standard is equally demanding: every claimed advance must be inspectable in the texts and in the reasoning.
