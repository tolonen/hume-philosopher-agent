## Exact target and reconstruction

Aaron Zimmerman, “Hume’s Reasons,” *Hume Studies* 33(2) (2007), 211–256, [original article](https://humesociety.org/uploads/issues/v33n2/383363.pdf), printed p. 219: “the conclusion of the argument must be that reason alone cannot cause action”. This is a claim about the argumentative role of T 2.3.3.5, not merely about Hume’s ultimate doctrine.

Zimmerman distinguishes causal impotence from normative criticism. His §1 reconstructs the representation argument through a truth-directed understanding, function-delimited powers, and passions’ lack of truth value. He supports that reconstruction with T 3.1.1.8–9 and Book 1’s psychology, then considers objections to its premises. Later sections defend non-instrumental practical reasons through reason combined with sympathy. His causal reading therefore does not deny reason an indirect role in action. At p. 222 he qualifies the production of passions, actions and volitions by reasoning; the remainder concerns actions deemed reasonable, prudent or moral. These qualifications must accompany the target. Scope: pp. 218–227; later argument and all notes, pp. 228–256, inspected to check qualifications and consequences.

## Objection version 1 and attempted defeat

**Target → evidence → premises → inference → proposed conclusion.**

1. Target: T 2.3.3.5 must establish reason’s independent motivational incapacity.
2. Evidence: its explicit explanatory clause defines contradiction in terms of disagreement between copied ideas and their objects. The following paragraph discusses when affections may be judged unreasonable.
3. Interpretive premise P1 (contested): argumentative opposition here is exclusively truth-evaluable contradiction; causal resistance is a separate relation.
4. Interpretive premise P2 (contested): confirmation of a paragraph’s opinion need not independently derive every preceding thesis.
5. Proposed inference: the representation paragraph could establish the category mistake in treating passion as contradictory without independently proving motivational impotence.
6. Proposed conclusion: a requirement that every adequate reading supply an independent causal derivation overstates what this paragraph does.

This initially looked like an objection to an imposed interpretive constraint. It was not a proof that Hume thought reason could independently motivate. A thesis can be true while one supposed proof of it fails.

**Strongest defence.** Read the whole sequence. T 2.3.3.4 introduces further confirmation after both causal claims and the slave metaphor. More decisively, T 3.1.1.8 introduces the representation discussion as the recollection of an inertness argument. T 3.1.1.10 explicitly assigns that argument an indirect motivational use. The later passage is in the same work’s next volume, not a much later Enquiry silently substituted for Book 2. The argumentative connection is Hume’s own. This does not establish every detail of functionalism, but it defeats the allegation that a motivational reading lacks textual grounding.

**Response and verdict.** P1 confused the local inferential machinery with the broader purpose it could serve; P2 was possible but insufficient against the explicit retrospective evidence. The initial objection is withdrawn. The distinction between contradiction and causal resistance remains useful, but cannot itself refute this target. Do not reopen it without new textual or scholarly evidence.

## Positive reading and remaining question

An interpretation that survives this investigation separates levels. Reason does not oppose a bare passion by proving the passion false. It can alter associated judgments and thus redirect or remove dependent motivations. The argument therefore attacks a model of rational government in which reason condemns an affection as a false representation and subdues it by that condemnation. Its motivational application draws on the surrounding account of impulses and judgment-dependent passions. This reading accommodates the retrospective Book 3 passage and the correction examples. It does not uniquely establish a modern faculty-functionalism or show that an anti-rationalist argument would persuade every opponent.

One residual question concerns the bridge from truth-directed function to incapacity. Consider two formal propositions: (a) some correctly functioning episodes of reasoning do not cause right action; (b) no episode of unaided reasoning can cause volition. Proposition (a) does not imply (b). A model with one non-motivating episode and one motivating episode satisfies (a) and falsifies (b). Nothing about this elementary countermodel establishes that Hume or Zimmerman endorsed it. It only shows that an insufficiency premise would need supplementation if used to prove incapacity.

The strongest repair is constitutive: independently initiating volition is excluded from the kind of operation identified as reasoning; causal effects on passion require another principle. That repair fits Hume’s distinction between immediate and mediate influence. But whether it answers the rationalist rather than defining the rationalist’s proposal away requires a separate assessment of the taxonomy and empirical evidence. This dossier records **a question about the adequacy of a reconstruction**, not a demonstrated historical error or a refuted conclusion. It does not treat an awkward qualification as the strongest reasonable version of the whole article.
