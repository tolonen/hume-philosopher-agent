## Exact target and its scope

Peter Millican, [“Hume, Causal Realism, and Causal Science,” *Mind* 118 (2009), 647–712](https://davidhume.org/scholarship/papers/millican/2009_Causal_Science.pdf), p.699 (PDF page 53), §8:

> “it is a role that requires them to be interpreted semantically rather than merely epistemologically”

This 15-word extract establishes an argumentative-requirement claim, not a misquotation claim. Its antecedent is the role of the two definitions in Hume's liberty-and-necessity arguments. Millican takes their force to exclude coherent reference to necessity beyond those definitions. His materialism discussion expressly distinguishes pragmatic warrant from conclusive determination (p.690); pp.691–692 challenge the former. The objection below must therefore address his reasons, rather than credit him with overlooking that distinction. His §4 also distinguishes genetic explanation from modern necessary-and-sufficient-condition analysis. The target is the inference from the definitions' argumentative employment to exhaustive thinkable content, not a claim that he simply confuses observation with metaphysics.

**Defeat condition:** construct an alternative that preserves the actual conclusion Hume asserts, the claimed compulsion of the argument, and the restriction of competing meanings, while leaving genuine causal necessity beyond the definitions intelligible. Mere compatibility with an attenuated conclusion will not suffice.

### Evidence favouring the proposed objection

[T 1.4.5.33, SBN 250](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/5#33), concludes a discussion that moves from substance and location to the cause of thought:

> To pronounce, then, the final decision upon the whole; the question concerning the substance of the soul is absolutely unintelligible: All our perceptions are not susceptible of a local union, either with what is extended or unextended; there being some of them of the one kind, and some of the other: And as the constant conjunction of objects constitutes the very essence of cause and effect, matter and motion may often be regarded as the causes of thought, as far as we have any notion of that relation.

The final qualification supplies a possible opening for distinguishing legitimate causal attribution from an unrestricted metaphysical assertion. Its earlier context is indispensable: [T 1.4.5.30, SBN 247–248](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/4/5#30) says that bodily dispositions change thoughts and concludes that motion actually causes thought; T 1.4.5.31–32 contrasts demanding a perceived connexion with treating constantly conjoined objects as causes. An alternative confined to agnosticism would omit the positive conclusion.

[T 1.3.14.27, SBN 168](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/14#27), in Hume's reply to the immediately preceding objection that power must be in objects:

> We do not understand our own meaning in talking so, but ignorantly confound ideas, which are entirely distinct from each other. I am, indeed, ready to allow, that there may be several qualities both in material and immaterial objects, with which we are utterly unacquainted; and if we please to call these power or efficacy, 'twill be of little consequence to the world. But when, instead of meaning these unknown qualities, we make the terms of power and efficacy signify something, of which we have a clear idea, and which is incompatible with those objects, to which we apply it, obscurity and error begin then to take place, and we are led astray by a false philosophy. This is the case, when we transfer the determination of the thought to external objects, and suppose any real intelligible connexion betwixt them; that being a quality, which can only belong to the mind that considers them.

This permits unknown qualities and nominal use of causal vocabulary for them. It does **not** identify those qualities as a further kind of intelligible causal necessity.

[T 2.3.2.4, SBN 409–410](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/2/3/2#4), answering the religious objection introduced in paragraph 3:

> Or that he will maintain there is something else in the operations of matter. Now whether it be so or not is of no consequence to religion, whatever it may be to natural philosophy. I may be mistaken in asserting, that we have no idea of any other connexion in the actions of body, and shall be glad to be farther instructed on that head: But sure I am, I ascribe nothing to the actions of the mind, but what must readily be allow'd of.

The next sentences explain the concession's function:

> I do not ascribe to the will that unintelligible necessity, which is suppos'd to lie in matter. But I ascribe to matter, that intelligible quality, call it necessity or not, which the most rigorous orthodoxy does or must allow to belong to the will. I change, therefore, nothing in the receiv'd systems, with regard to the will, but only with regard to material objects.

That restricted religious defence remains available even if the earlier conceptual argument fails. It is not an unambiguous retraction of the earlier argument.

### Evidence favouring the scholar's defence

[T 1.3.14.33, SBN 171](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/1/3/14#33):

> Secondly, The same course of reasoning will make us conclude, that there is but one kind of necessity, as there is but one kind of cause, and that the common distinction betwixt moral and physical necessity is without any foundation in nature. This clearly appears from the precedent explication of necessity. 'Tis the constant conjunction of objects, along with the determination of the mind, which constitutes a physical necessity: And the removal of these is the same thing with chance.

The paragraph continues that differing degrees of constancy do not produce a different species of the relation. This is a positive claim about the distinction's foundation in nature, not merely about the evidential symmetry between two unobserved relations.

[T 2.3.1.4, SBN 400–401](https://davidhume.org/texts/t/2/3/1#4), follows the proposed comparison with necessary material action:

> Here then are two particulars, which we are to consider as essential to necessity, viz. the constant union and the inference of the mind; and wherever we discover these we must acknowledge a necessity. As the actions of matter have no necessity, but what is deriv'd from these circumstances, and it is not by any insight into the essence of bodies we discover their connexion, the absence of this insight, while the union and inference remain, will never, in any case, remove the necessity.

[A 34, SBN 661](https://davidhume.org/texts/a/#34), following A 31–33's comparison of matter and human action:

> Our author pretends, that this reasoning puts the whole controversy in a new light, by giving a new definition of necessity. And, indeed, the most zealous advocates for free-will must allow this union and inference with regard to human actions. They will only deny, that this makes the whole of necessity. But then they must shew, that we have an idea of something else in the actions of matter; which, according to the foregoing reasoning, is impossible.

[E 8.5, SBN 82](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/8#5) similarly says that the two circumstances exhaust the necessity ascribed to matter and that we possess no further notion. E 8.6 infers universal agreement; E 8.27 repeats the religious defence while conceding possible error concerning another idea of material necessity. Its placement in Part II prevents using it silently to reinterpret the whole preceding proof as only a modest evidential claim.

## Reconstructable chain and interpretive premises

1. **Target:** the definitions' role requires an exhaustive semantic constraint rather than merely an epistemic one.
2. **Shared primary premise S1:** human and physical phenomena exhibit conjunction and support inference; T 2.3.1.4 and A 31–34 apply the same two particulars to both.
3. **Shared primary premise S2:** Hume challenges his opponent to provide another idea; A 34 does not simply ask for better observations.
4. **Contested premise C1:** an idea's impression-source exhausts the content to which it can refer. This is an interpretive claim about representation, not a tautology derived from S1.
5. **Contested premise C2:** Hume's conclusion is the identity of the relevant kinds of necessity, including rejection of a further physical kind, rather than only parity in legitimate causal judgement.
6. **Proposed objection:** S1–S2 could support uniform legitimate ascription without showing that the ascribed concept reduces the external relation to regularity. A concept might have one source and one use across domains while referring to an underlying feature whose nature is not fully grasped.
7. **Defence:** to retain C2 that feature must not support an intelligible distinction between physical and moral necessity. Simply allowing unknown relations does not prevent the opponent from making precisely that distinction. The objection therefore needs an independently justified representation theory that preserves univocity while closing the conceptual space for the rival distinction.
8. **Precise verdict:** the conceptual counterpossibility establishes a distinction between univocity and reduction. It does not establish that Hume accepts the required representation theory, nor that the weaker reading preserves his asserted conclusion. The attempted refutation fails at that step.

No formal theorem prover was used. The schematic countermodel is a diagnostic of the interpretive premise, not a machine-verified account of Hume.

## Strongest objection, defence, and response record

**O1 — Evidential sufficiency entails less than reduction.** From the fact that conjunction warrants asserting a cause, no general metaphysical reduction follows. **Defence:** this concerns a weaker warrant argument; the actual primary text includes essence language, the denial of a foundation in nature, and a challenge to the opponent's idea. **Response:** valid abstract distinction; unsuccessful local refutation. Withdraw the diagnosis that the scholar overlooked it.

**O2 — The unknown-qualities concession defeats semantic exhaustiveness.** T 1.3.14.27 allows qualities outside our acquaintance to be called powers. **Defence:** the passage distinguishes those nominal unknown qualities from the intelligible connexion rejected in its following sentences. **Response:** the concession blocks an overbroad claim that Hume forbids all supposition of any unknown quality. It does not establish the disputed causal reference. No misquotation or contradiction demonstrated.

**O3 — The religious concession leaves metaphysical difference open.** T 2.3.2.4 and E 8.27 allow a defence independently of strict exhaustiveness. **Defence:** an additional defence of innocence does not retract the primary doctrinal proof. **Response:** supports a restricted conclusion about that subargument; fails as a refutation of the intended doctrinal argument.

**O4 — Semantic univocity could coexist with non-reductive reference.** This is the strongest alternative, but it depends on a theory of an impression-derived idea representing more than its felt content. **Defence and later qualification:** [Millican 2011, “Hume, Causal Realism, and Free Will,” pp.133–142](https://davidhume.org/scholarship/papers/millican/2011_Hume_Causal_Realism_Free_Will.pdf), directly examines Beebee's proposal, distinguishes phenomenological character from representative content, rejects normative symmetry as insufficient against the Clarkean distinction, and challenges the textual foundation of extended reference. At pp.141–142 he conditionally acknowledges that a successful representation account could reconcile her reading; the decisive objection would then concern her version of realism rather than the liberty argument specifically. **Response:** this conditional limitation should govern our report. It is not an unacknowledged omission newly found by HPA.

## Alternative reading and its limits

The viable competing reading treats the argument as enforcing one legitimate causal concept and one policy for its application in material and human cases. Conjunction and inference license necessity claims in both; the opponent cannot use necessity in one sense for matter and another for minds without providing a further intelligible idea. A single causal concept need not, as a matter of logic, describe its referent exhaustively. Unknown qualities may exist; they cannot establish the rival physical/moral distinction just by being renamed.

This explains the qualifications about our notion, the pattern of empirical comparisons, and the demand for an alternative idea. It also preserves a substantive assertion of causation rather than translating all causal claims into mere uncertainty. Its unresolved burden is explaining why the representation of an unknown causal feature cannot underwrite different kinds of necessity, while accounting for T 1.3.14.33's unqualified claim about nature. Adopting this reading would shift the argument from a metaphysical reduction to restrictions on legitimate causal content and use. We have not established that Hume makes that shift.

## Novelty, replies, and later revisions

[Helen Beebee, “The Two Definitions and the Doctrine of Necessity,” *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 107 (2007), 413–431](https://philpapers.org/archive/BEETTD.pdf), especially pp.425–430, already distinguishes a weak evidential argument from a reconstruction involving natural belief, common causal content, and norms of application. She acknowledges the former is insufficient and offers the latter. The present objection repeats that published family of arguments rather than improving its textual proof. Her full article, including references, was inspected.

The 2009 target already replies in note 61, p.700. The 2011 reply's §IV was inspected selectively across pp.133–142; it was not read as a whole 43-page chapter. [Millican 2024, “Hume as Regularity Theorist—After All! Completing a Counter-Revolution,” *Hume Studies* 49, 101–162](https://davidhume.org/scholarship/papers/millican/2024_Hume_Regularity_Theorist.pdf), was inspected selectively: abstract, retrospective discussion around pp.118–119, definitional treatment around pp.125–131, subjectivism discussion around pp.135–140, conclusion around pp.148–149, and relevant reference notes. It revises earlier subjectivist emphases, gives priority to the regularity criterion, and acknowledges an inadequately argued transition from psychological origin to normative application. It retains the restriction on alternative causal thought. That acknowledgement concerns Hume's argumentative success; it is not a concession that the 2009 interpretation has been refuted.
