Reorient the Hume Philosopher Agent (HPA) towards systematic reconstruction and testing of Hume’s philosophy across his works and over time.

The previous investigations produced useful distinctions and withdrew weak objections. But the positive reconstruction still developed largely by finding passages compatible with an emerging proposal. We now need to investigate the premises themselves, construct competing accounts of how Hume’s arguments work, and determine what those accounts explain, require and fail to explain.

Our aim is a substantive philosophical contribution: a new solution, a decisive objection, or a consequential revision of an existing dispute. We also want to establish how far an LLM can advance history of philosophy through sustained reasoning. Build the argument through the primary corpus and original scholarship. A fitting passage is a starting point, not a completed demonstration.

**1. Reopen the current reconstruction at the level of its premises.**

Treat the existing HPA dossiers as provisional research, including the proposal that Hume licenses causal claims while restricting further metaphysical commitments.

Identify every premise needed to move from that proposal to conclusions about external objects, other minds, composition, personal identity and rational assent. Distinguish premises Hume states, premises his arguments require, premises scholars supply, and premises we introduced.

In particular, test these transitions:

- Does ignorance of a substance’s nature permit suspension about its existence?
- Does explaining experience without a metaphysical premise establish that the premise is unnecessary for evidential warrant?
- Does warranted inference about another person require commitment to an independently existing body or mind?
- Does causal connectedness establish unity, persistence or one consciousness?
- Does an affirmative claim express belief, rational endorsement, practical reliance or reasoning under a supposition?
- Does successful explanatory practice establish any stronger ontological commitment?

These are questions to resolve. Identify the missing bridge wherever a conclusion does not follow.

**2. Build a structured account of the primary corpus.**

Reuse the preserved corpus and existing infrastructure. Establish actual coverage across the Treatise and Appendix, Abstract, both Enquiries, essays, dissertations, Dialogues, History and correspondence. Include relevant textual variants and revisions.

For active problems, read complete argumentative sections and their dependencies. Search the remaining works for relevant reasoning, applications and counterexamples, including evidence outside the vocabulary normally used in the debate.

Preserve genre, argumentative voice and context. Distinguish direct assertions, reported opinions, methodological suppositions, objections and arguments assigned to dialogue speakers. Explain the bridge when historical explanation or another genre supplies evidence for a philosophical interpretation.

Record what was indexed, searched, read in context and reconstructed. Corpus availability does not establish corpus comprehension. Do not claim that all Hume has been modelled because all files are searchable.

**3. Model intellectual development explicitly.**

Do not combine statements from different periods into a single timeless “Hume”. Build chronological versions of the argument model, distinguishing composition, publication and revision dates wherever possible. Preserve uncertainty where the evidence cannot establish the sequence.

Our particular interest includes earlier positions Hume later qualified, revised or abandoned. Establish what those positions originally meant, how they supported surrounding arguments, and what modifying them did to the structure of his thinking.

Reconstruct an earlier argument first using evidence from its own setting. Identify its premises, conclusion, dependencies and unresolved problems before bringing later formulations into the assessment. Later texts may illuminate an earlier position, but must not silently replace it.

For each apparent change, distinguish:

- Explicit withdrawal or correction.
- Modification of a premise, conclusion or scope.
- A different argument for a retained conclusion.
- Changed terminology or presentation.
- A change of audience, genre or argumentative purpose.
- Omission without established repudiation.
- Continuing tension or unresolved inconsistency.

Retreat is one developmental hypothesis among others. Omission, or Hume’s later preference for another work, does not establish that every omitted doctrine was abandoned.

Preserve the original Treatise and Appendix interventions as distinct stages. Examine relevant revisions between editions of later works. Editorially integrated additions must not erase the development under investigation.

When a change is established, trace its consequences: which other arguments depended on the earlier premise? Did Hume revise them, replace their support, retain them on different grounds, or leave a problem unresolved? Distinguish documented revisions from consequences we derive.

Use dated correspondence and other evidence where relevant, keeping private statements, published arguments and retrospective descriptions distinct.

**4. Construct a model of arguments and dependencies.**

Represent propositions, their scope, premises, inferential connections and competing interpretations. The model must support reasoning rather than merely organise quotations.

For each important proposition record:

- Its exact source, textual state and argumentative context.
- Who asserts it and under what conditions.
- Its meaning in that context.
- Its supporting premises and consequences.
- Relevant qualifications, objections and apparent conflicts.
- Interpretive alternatives and the evidence that distinguishes them.

Represent dependencies involving several premises together. One passage may supply a premise while another restricts its application. Track whether a dependency belongs to Hume’s explicit reasoning or our reconstruction.

Begin with connected questions about perception, belief, reason, causal inference, objects, persons, composition, identity, scepticism and assent. Bring passions, sympathy, moral judgment and historical explanation into the model wherever they impose relevant constraints.

Do not impose global consistency. The model must accommodate genuine tension, local commitments and intellectual change where the evidence supports them.

**5. Reconstruct rival scholarly positions as complete arguments.**

Return to the original arguments of Garrett, Hakkarainen, Baxter and the relevant interlocutors already identified. Resolve important access gaps where possible. Do not attribute a position from a title, summary or another scholar’s criticism.

For each central dispute, establish:

- What the strongest rival accounts agree on.
- Their exact disagreement about premises, meanings or inference.
- What each account commits Hume to elsewhere.
- How each handles difficult primary evidence.
- What would defeat or materially weaken it.

Broad labels such as realism, scepticism or perspectivalism are insufficient. Scholars using similar labels may defend different commitments.

Construct another alternative when a dispute rests on a questionable shared premise. Explain whether it resolves the disagreement, changes its terms or simply adds another viable interpretation.

Reconstruct rival developmental explanations too. Identify what each explains about earlier and later arguments, what textual pattern it predicts, and what evidence counts against it.

**6. Derive consequences and test them across the corpus.**

For every serious interpretation, derive consequences before searching for support.

Ask what the interpretation implies for another relevant argument. Test an account of causal warrant against testimony, other minds and religious inference; test composition against spatial complexes, changing objects and personal identity. Explain why the connection holds rather than assuming that similar words perform identical functions.

Maintain a consequence record:

- Interpretation and explicit premises.
- Consequence that follows.
- Primary argument used to test it.
- Outcome and strongest rival explanation.
- Revision required, if any.

Distinguish commitments implied by an interpretation from conclusions Hume explicitly articulates. A historical author need not state every consequence of a position.

Seek counterexamples, limiting cases and contrary evidence. When an interpretation needs an exception, determine whether Hume motivates it or whether we introduced it to protect the interpretation.

Reserve additional relevant passages, where practicable, for testing a reconstruction after its consequences have been stated. Record subsequent changes rather than presenting a revised account as if it predicted everything from the beginning.

**7. Use formal reasoning where it clarifies the disagreement.**

Where a dispute turns on entailment, necessity, possibility, identity or part–whole relations, formalise the argument sufficiently to test it.

Explain each formal term’s correspondence to the text. Keep “Hume accepts P” distinct from P being true, and permission or compatibility distinct from commitment. Do not collapse conceivability, possibility, belief and knowledge.

Use proof checking or model finding where useful. Seek countermodels in which the stated premises hold while the disputed conclusion fails. A countermodel may establish that another premise is required; it does not automatically refute an interpretation.

For contradictions, identify the smallest conflicting premise set. Test alternative translations, restrictions of scope, chronology and argumentative voice. Preserve genuine conflicts when those checks do not resolve them.

Formal tools establish consequences of a reconstruction. Its fidelity to Hume requires separate textual evidence. Do not introduce a contested interpretation as an axiom and then describe its consequences as a proof of that interpretation.

**8. Complete the first integrated philosophical investigation.**

Use causal inquiry, metaphysical commitment, composition and assent as the first connected test.

Determine whether a defensible account explains how Hume makes positive claims about bodies and other people, limits those claims, treats objects as complex or persisting, explains belief, evaluates its authority, and describes scepticism.

Establish which questions share premises and which require independent arguments. Do not assume one principle must resolve everything.

The account must explain why the limits fall where they do. If ordinary empirical inference receives warrant unavailable to theological inference, identify the operative difference and derive its consequences. If relations among parts suffice for explanation but not for unity or consciousness, establish why.

Reconstruct the earlier positions with particular care. Investigate whether later changes expose an earlier problem, remove a premise, preserve a conclusion by another route or alter the problem itself.

The result may be a coherent solution, a consequential developmental reconstruction, or a demonstration that the existing binary dispute rests on a false premise. It must go beyond listing distinctions.

**9. Subject the reconstruction to sustained opposition.**

Use separate assessments to defend the strongest rival positions and attack our premises, formalisation, chronological account and treatment of counterevidence. Supply the relevant texts and complete arguments.

Require concrete objections: unsupported bridges, neglected arguments, equivocations, untenable consequences or better rival explanations. Agreement between agents is not independent historical evidence.

Resolve objections in the substantive reconstruction. Do not repeatedly weaken the conclusion until it becomes unobjectionable but uninformative. If the central claim fails, replace it and explain what the failure establishes.

An invalid scholarly inference does not automatically establish a false conclusion. A stronger alternative reading must earn its own support.

**10. Establish contribution and limits.**

Compare the strongest argument with existing scholarship on the relevant disputes. A new combination of familiar passages is valuable only if it establishes something consequential that the existing accounts do not.

Identify exactly what changes: a premise, inference, doctrinal attribution, developmental explanation, relationship between problems, or account of an apparent contradiction.

Preserve unsuccessful models and the reasons they failed. State when multiple interpretations remain viable. Record the scope of the novelty search; failure to find a precedent does not prove originality.

Label automated assessments accurately and make the argument inspectable by a philosopher. Neither citation verification nor formal validity alone certifies the interpretation.

**11. Execute and deliver a complete reconstruction cycle.**

Begin the substantive work using the existing infrastructure. Build the model while completing the integrated investigation; do not postpone philosophical reasoning until an unlimited corpus project is finished.

The next delivery must contain:

- The revised central question.
- Competing models and their disputed premises.
- A connected argument across relevant works and textual stages.
- The earlier position reconstructed in its own terms.
- Evidence for subsequent changes and their structural consequences.
- Consequence tests, counterexamples and useful formal results.
- The strongest surviving positive reconstruction.
- Its strongest objection and the response.
- Its relationship to existing scholarship and actual reading coverage.

Include a sustained written argument a philosopher can evaluate without assembling it from scattered dossiers. The supporting model and records must expose how its conclusions were reached.
